TopicSydney Cove: one harbour, two beginnings

Lecture 03 · Colonisation Gadigal CountrySYDNEY19–23 Sept 2026

Description

The Sydney Opera House sits on Bennelong Point, which the Gadigal people of the Eora Nation know as Tubowgule. A few hundred metres away is Sydney Cove (Warrane), where the First Fleet's eleven ships landed on 26 January 1788 and Arthur Phillip founded a British penal colony. The point is named for Woollarawarre Bennelong, a Wangal man who was kidnapped on Phillip's orders and later became a go-between for the Eora and the colonists. I photographed it at dusk, after dark and, less gracefully, two miles into a morning run.

Personal reflection

Every postcard of Australia is taken from roughly where I was standing, so I expected to feel like a tourist. What I felt was the course's first big idea landing. This is the exact place where two incompatible stories about the same land met. To the British, the harbour was terra nullius: land belonging to no one in any way their law would recognise. To the Eora it was Country, already full of law, names and obligations. Our lecture estimated that about 70 per cent of the Eora died in the smallpox epidemic that followed. None of that is visible from the forecourt, where the main hazards are seagulls and selfie sticks.

What surprised me is how openly Australia argues about this. The 26th of January is a public holiday that some people celebrate as Australia Day and others mourn as Invasion Day, and the argument happens out loud every year. At home, the Fourth of July marks a revolution against Britain, which lets Americans cast themselves as the underdogs of their own founding. Australia has no such escape hatch: its national day marks the arrival of the empire. I think that makes the founding harder to romanticise here, and I mean that as a compliment.

It also made me look harder at my own campus. Stanford sits on the ancestral land of the Muwekma Ohlone, a sentence I have heard read aloud at events without really hearing it. In Sydney the older name, Tubowgule, is printed right beside the newer one. I would like to get better at noticing both.

TopicBondi: the beach as a national myth

Lecture 09 · Values & icons Surf reportBONDI21 Sept 2026

Description

Bondi is Australia's most famous kilometre of sand. Its name comes from an Aboriginal word usually translated as the sound of water breaking over rocks. The Bondi Surf Bathers' Life Saving Club, founded in 1907, claims to be the oldest in the world, and volunteer lifesavers in red and yellow still mark the safe swimming zone with flags. Our lecture listed the beach among Australia's everyday icons, beside the Hills Hoist and the democracy sausage, and noted that about 87 per cent of Australians live within 50 km of the coast. We took our surfboards there on a public bus.

Personal reflection

I come from the country that put surfing in the movies, so I did not expect to be out-beached. The difference showed up before we reached the water: we carried surfboards onto a city bus and nobody blinked. The beach here is treated as public infrastructure, something like a library with rip currents. Nobody owns the sand, the lifesavers are volunteers and the showers are free.

The course taught me to read that as a claim about values. The beach is where the "fair go" is supposed to be most visible, because a barrister and a bricklayer in board shorts wait for the same wave. California tells a similar story about itself, and its law even agrees: the coast is public. In practice, a short drive from Stanford, a billionaire has spent more than a decade in court trying to keep people off Martins Beach. Australia seems to have mostly won an argument that California is still having.

But the lecture also asked who an icon leaves out, and Bondi has answers. The bronzed lifesaver of the posters is white and male. In 2005 the Cronulla riots, on a beach just down the coast, showed how quickly "our beach" can be turned against people who look like recent migrants. And the oldest claim to this shoreline is in the name Bondi itself. I still think the beach was the most democratic place I visited in Australia. I just no longer assume that "democratic" and "for everyone" mean the same thing. My own surfing, for the record, was a fair go and not much more.

TopicHawke's Lager: a prime minister on a beer can

Lecture 09 · The fair go Hill aboveBONDI21 Sept 2026
"Any boss who sacks anyone for not turning up today is a bum."Bob Hawke, PM · America's Cup, 1983

Description

This is a can of Hawke's Lager, photographed on the hill above Bondi. The face on it belongs to Bob Hawke, the trade union leader who was Prime Minister from 1983 to 1991. Hawke was famous for setting a world record for drinking a yard of ale as a student at Oxford, and for announcing on live television, the morning Australia won the America's Cup in 1983, that "any boss who sacks anyone for not turning up today is a bum." The brewery launched in 2017 with his blessing, and he gave his share to Landcare, the conservation movement he launched nationally as Prime Minister. Hence the line on the can: "Every sip gives back."

Personal reflection

I bought this because it was funny: a head of government, grinning on a lager. Then I spent the afternoon thinking about why it would be impossible at home.

Americans do put politicians on things, but only once they are safely marble. Samuel Adams got his beer two centuries after he died. Presidents go on currency and mountains, and we expect them to behave like people who will one day be on currency and mountains. Hawke got his beer while he was alive, and the achievement it winks at is drinking one very quickly.

That is the larrikin from our lecture on national identity: affection for someone irreverent, anti-authoritarian and unimpressed by rank, in a line the course traced from the Eureka diggers through Ned Kelly. It travels with the "fair go". A leader may be powerful as long as he never acts as though he is better than you. Australians call their prime ministers by nicknames. American presidents get "Hail to the Chief".

I find the Australian version more appealing, and I am suspicious of how appealing I find it. The larrikin is a specific kind of person: male, white, fond of a drink. The lecture pointed out that the bush legend left out women, First Nations people and non-British migrants, and the bloke on the can inherits that. A beer record made Hawke charming. I doubt the same charm was on offer to Julia Gillard. Still, "every sip gives back" may be the most Australian sentence I read all quarter: do something for the common good, but don't be earnest about it.

TopicThe pub: mateship, sport and the punt

Lecture 10 · Sporting traditions Friday nightSYDNEY18 Sept 2026

BETTING SLIP

Wagered, 2022–23
$244.3b
…through pokies
$191.1b
Melbourne Cup
1st Tue Nov

Figures from Lecture 10. Totals wagered, not losses. Gamble responsibly.

worst photo, best evidence

Description

A pub in central Sydney on a Friday night in September. Behind us, a bank of screens is showing horse racing. Our sport lecture described how British games were imported and remade in Australia, and how gambling grew up beside them: Australians wagered about $244 billion in 2022–23, $191 billion of it through poker machines, the "pokies" found in pubs and clubs across most of the country. The Melbourne Cup, run on the first Tuesday of November, is still called "the race that stops a nation".

Personal reflection

This is the worst photo in the diary and one of the most useful. I took it because we were having a great night. Only later did I notice what was on the screens behind us: horse racing, at ten at night, in a room where nobody appeared to be watching it.

Pubs were where I saw the course's idea of mateship most plainly. Drinks are bought in rounds, called "shouts", and the system only works if everyone takes a turn, which makes it a small weekly exercise in egalitarianism. Compared with an American bar, a pub is also less of a single-age space. Many have a bistro and a beer garden, and families come in for lunch.

But the living room has a casino in it. In the United States, gambling has traditionally been a destination: you go to Las Vegas, and what happens there stays there. Here it is part of the furniture, and the lecture's numbers suggest what that costs. I can't be smug about it, though. Since 2018, when the Supreme Court let the states legalise sports betting, American broadcasts have filled up with betting ads, and anyone at home with a phone can now lose money from the couch. Australia is no exotic exception. It may be a preview.

What I take from the lecture is that sport works as a mirror. The culture that produced Cathy Freeman's 400 metres and the Matildas' World Cup run also produced the pokies room. Both come from loving sport and the company around it. They differ in who profits and who pays.

TopicThe Chinese Garden of Friendship: from poll tax to sister-state

Lectures 05 & 07 · Gold & migration Darling HarbourSYDNEY19 Sept 2026

Description

The Chinese Garden of Friendship is a walled garden in Darling Harbour, on the edge of Sydney's Chinatown, with pavilions and willows backed by glass towers. It opened in 1988, and the plaque at my feet says it "celebrates the sister-state relationship of China's Guangdong province and the New South Wales Government." Guangdong is also where most of the Chinese miners on the 1850s goldfields came from. They were the largest non-European group on the diggings, and the colonies answered with a £10 arrival tax and, after Federation, with the Immigration Restriction Act 1901 and its dictation test.

Personal reflection

I liked the garden first and thought about it second. It is quiet in a way the middle of a city of five million should not be, and the skyline over the pavilion roof is the best picture of multiculturalism I found in Sydney, because neither one cancels the other out.

Then I read the plaque again. "Friendship" is doing a lot of work. The province whose artisans helped build this garden is the one whose miners were taxed on arrival, pushed around the goldfields and then written out of the new nation in 1901. Our migration lecture traced the long turn from White Australia, through assimilation and its "New Australians", to official multiculturalism in the 1970s. The garden is that last stage in physical form: a government paying to make a migrant culture visible instead of asking it to disappear.

The American parallel is uncomfortably close, and for me it is local. California had its own gold rush just before Australia's, its own Foreign Miners' Tax, and then the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882. Chinese labourers built the western half of the transcontinental railroad, and the fortune from that railroad built my university, which only began formally honouring those workers in the 2010s. Both countries built wealth on Chinese labour, banned Chinese migration, and now put up gardens and plaques.

The language differs. Americans say "melting pot", which is assimilation with better branding, since everyone ends up as the same soup. Australia's official word, multiculturalism, at least promises that the ingredients stay recognisable. Whether daily life keeps that promise is another matter, and the lecture was honest about the backlash. But I trust a country a little more when its plaque and its history can be read side by side, even if the plaque only tells the cheerful half.

TopicThe Blue Mountains: Country, not wilderness

Lecture 02 · Aboriginal Australia Gundungurra & Dharug CountryKATOOMBA29–30 Sept

Description

The Blue Mountains rise about 100 km west of Sydney. They are the Country of the Gundungurra and Dharug peoples and, since 2000, part of the Greater Blue Mountains World Heritage Area, listed largely for the diversity of its eucalypts. The blue haze comes from fine droplets of eucalyptus oil scattering the light. We hiked the cliff tracks, found a waterfall with a swimming hole and watched the sun go down over the valley from Katoomba. In the Black Summer of 2019–20, fire burned through most of this World Heritage Area; nationally, our lecture put the toll at about 19 million hectares and 3 billion animals.

Personal reflection

My first reaction at this lookout was the standard one: untouched wilderness as far as I could see. The course had already ruined that reaction for me, and I am grateful.

The lecture on Aboriginal Australia argued that this landscape was never untouched. Country is a relationship that joins land, ancestors, law and the duty to care for a place. For thousands of years that care included fire: cool, patchy, deliberate burning suited to the season and the terrain. Bill Gammage called the result "the biggest estate on earth". What looked like wilderness to the British was closer to a garden whose gardeners they were pushing out. When those fire regimes were broken, fuel built up, and it is hard to stand here six years after Black Summer without connecting the two.

This is where Australia stopped feeling like a foreign country and started feeling like a mirror. I go to school in California, where "fire season" is now part of the calendar. American national parks were founded on the same idea of wilderness. Yosemite was celebrated as pristine after the Ahwahneechee had been driven out of it, and a century of putting out every fire left the forests primed to burn. Tribes such as the Karuk and Yurok are now leading the return of cultural burning there, much as Indigenous rangers are here.

The bush legend treats the bush as an opponent that a tough settler survives. Looking at this valley, I prefer the older idea, that land is something you owe things to. The dead tree in the foreground is why I kept this photo. It reads as damage at first. In a eucalypt forest it is also part of how the place works.

TopicHeron Island: the reef, and who looks after it

Lecture 12 · Conservation Great Barrier ReefHERON IS.2–12 Oct 2026

Description

Heron Island is a coral cay at the southern end of the Great Barrier Reef, about 80 km off Gladstone and small enough to walk around in half an hour. It lies inside a national park and the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, a World Heritage Area since 1981, and it hosts a University of Queensland research station. Turtles feed on the reef (one photobombed my snorkel selfie), rays cruise the shallows after dark, and the sky is dark enough to photograph the Milky Way on a phone. This is the Sea Country of the Bailai, Gurang, Gooreng Gooreng and Taribelang Bunda peoples.

Personal reflection

I have never been anywhere that made conservation feel less abstract. On Heron you cannot treat nature as scenery, because you are standing in it up to your neck. The turtle behind me in this photo did not care that I was there, which I took as the highest possible compliment to the people who manage the place.

Our last lecture argued that conservation is a negotiation among ecological, cultural and economic values, not a line drawn around something pretty. The reef is the clearest case I can think of. It is zoned, monitored and loved, and it sits downstream of the national economy. Gladstone, where the boat to Heron leaves from, is one of the world's big coal and gas ports. Australia protects the reef locally while exporting the fuel that warms it globally, and the mass bleaching events of the past decade show which side is winning. A marine park boundary is no use against water temperature.

I can't feel superior about that. The United States has made the same bargain on a larger scale, and Stanford's own marine station on Monterey Bay studies an ocean that my country's emissions are changing. What I did notice is a difference in who gets a say. American national parks were built on the idea of nature without people. On the reef, Traditional Owners are increasingly written into management as partners, through Indigenous rangers and Sea Country agreements. That is the course's idea of Country turned into policy. It came late and it is incomplete, but it treats knowledge that is thousands of years old as an asset.

The Milky Way photo is here for scale. Heron made me feel small twice: once underwater, and once looking up.

TopicK'gari: an island gets its name back

Lecture 08 · Rights & land Butchulla CountryK'GARI16–17 Oct 2026
Butchulla lore
  1. What is good for the land comes first.
  2. Do not take or touch anything that does not belong to you.
  3. If you have plenty, you must share.

Description

K'gari (say "gurri") is the world's largest sand island, with rainforest growing straight out of the dunes. It is Butchulla Country, and in the Butchulla language the name means paradise. For more than a century the maps said Fraser Island, after Eliza Fraser, who was shipwrecked here in 1836. The Federal Court recognised the Butchulla people's native title in 2014, and in 2023 the Queensland Government officially restored the name K'gari. The island's dingoes, called wongari, are protected, and visitors are told to keep their distance.

Personal reflection

I arrived knowing this island by the wrong name. Fraser Island is what the older maps and half the internet still call it. By the time we left, the old name was the one that sounded strange.

That small switch is a version of what our land rights lecture described. Terra nullius worked by making existing relationships to land invisible, and naming was one of its tools: call a place after a shipwrecked Englishwoman and its much older history drops off the map. Mabo overturned the legal fiction in 1992, the Native Title Act followed in 1993, and the Butchulla won their determination in 2014. Restoring a name does not hand back an island, and native title is a weaker right than ownership. But it changes what every visitor says out loud.

The comparison I kept reaching for was Denali. Alaska's highest peak carried the name of President McKinley, who never saw it, until the Koyukon name was restored in 2015. In 2025 a new administration ordered it changed back. That reversal taught me something about K'gari. A restored name is no finish line. It lasts only as long as the politics that restored it. Australians voted No to the Voice in 2023 and Queensland repealed its Path to Treaty the following year, so the ground is not settled here either.

The first Butchulla law is "What is good for the land comes first." I have been turning that over since October. American law tends to start from the other end, with what an owner is entitled to do. I can't think of a sentence like it in my country's founding documents, and I think we are poorer for that.

TopicMinjerribah: a flag on a pole

Lecture 11 · Reconciliation Quandamooka CountryMINJERRIBAH6 Nov 2026
the people
the sun
the earth
HAROLD THOMAS, 1971

Description

Minjerribah, also known as North Stradbroke Island or "Straddie", is Quandamooka Country in Moreton Bay, a ferry ride from Brisbane. In 2011 the Federal Court recognised the Quandamooka people's native title over much of the island, and sand mining, which had run here for decades, ended in 2019. Near Point Lookout I found a roadside pole painted as the Aboriginal flag, designed by Luritja artist Harold Thomas in 1971: black for the people, red for the earth and a yellow sun. On this one, somebody has painted a dollar sign inside the sun. A minute down the road, a sign warns drivers about koalas and kangaroos.

Personal reflection

I can't tell you what the dollar sign means, which is why this is my favourite photo in the diary. I have three readings. It could be a protest about the island, which was mined for its sand for decades while most of the money left. It could be about the flag itself, whose copyright was privately held until the Commonwealth paid about $20 million in 2022 so that anyone could fly it. Or it could be aimed at people like me, visitors who treat Aboriginal culture as something to buy on the way to the beach. All three would be fair.

Whichever it is, the pole made the reconciliation lecture concrete. The course described five dimensions of reconciliation, among them historical acceptance and institutional integrity, and stressed that it is the opposite of assimilation: a relationship between peoples who remain distinct. A flag on a pole is recognition. A dollar sign in the flag asks what the recognition is worth. After the Voice referendum failed in 2023, that question feels very alive in Queensland.

My other coursework this quarter kept bringing me back to Quandamooka Sea Country, and what struck me was how ordinary Indigenous presence is in Australian public life. The Aboriginal flag is an official flag of Australia and flies above the Sydney Harbour Bridge. Lectures begin with an Acknowledgement of Country. The United States has very little of that visibility, and at first I thought Australia was simply ahead.

It is more tangled than that. The United States signed hundreds of treaties with Native nations, broke most of them, and still recognises tribes as sovereign governments. Australia has never concluded a national treaty. America has the legal structure without the daily recognition, and Australia has the recognition without the structure. Each country is doing the half the other finds hard.

Draft note for BenjiDid anyone on Straddie (QYAC, a ranger, your instructor) explain the pole? Your oyster-reef and BRUV projects were on Quandamooka Sea Country: tell me who you worked with and one thing they taught you, and I'll replace my vague third paragraph.

TopicLamington: the bush legend meets a leech

Lecture 09 · The bush legend Yugambeh CountryLAMINGTON N.P.26–28 Oct 2026

Description

Lamington National Park sits on the rim of an ancient volcano on the Queensland–New South Wales border, in Yugambeh Country. Protected since 1915, it is part of the Gondwana Rainforests of Australia World Heritage Area, whose Antarctic beech trees descend from forests that grew when Australia was still joined to Antarctica. The first photo is the view from the escarpment. The second, from the same week, is a leech being removed from an ankle with a travel money card, which is the approved method: slide something flat under the sucker, and don't panic.

Personal reflection

Americans mostly know Australian wildlife as a list of things that can kill you. What actually got us was two centimetres long and was defeated with a debit card.

I have put it next to the view on purpose. Our identity lecture described the bush legend: the tough, practical, dry-humoured bushman of Henry Lawson and Banjo Paterson, later canonised by Russel Ward, who survives a hostile land with his mates. Watching Stanford students deal with one leech, I understood both the legend's appeal and its exaggeration. Nobody was in danger. But the response was mateship in miniature: one person held still and another operated. The harsh-environment story is mostly a story about how people treat each other when something goes slightly wrong.

America has its own version in the frontier and the cowboy, and the two legends differ in a way I find revealing. The American hero is a rugged individual who conquers the wilderness, alone, and gets rich. The Australian one endures it, with friends, and makes a joke. One legend is about winning and the other is about coping, which may be what happens when a national myth begins with convicts instead of pilgrims.

Both legends leave out the same people. This forest was Yugambeh Country long before it was a national park or a proving ground for bushmen, and the lecture's point was that the legend had no room for First Nations knowledge or for women's work. Most Australians also live in suburbs near the coast and meet the bush as we did: on a weekend, on a marked track, with snacks. I don't think that makes the legend false. It is a story a country tells about itself, like ours, and I liked it better for the leech.

Last page

The question I am taking home is the one the course kept asking: whose story is this, and who got left out of it?

It worked on a harbour, a beer can, a garden and a painted pole. I expect it will work on California too.
