TopicSydney Cove

Gadigal CountrySYDNEY19–23 Sept 2026

Description

The Sydney Opera House sits on Bennelong Point, which the Gadigal people of the Eora Nation know as Tubowgule. A few hundred metres away is Sydney Cove (Warrane), where the First Fleet's eleven ships landed on 26 January 1788 and Arthur Phillip founded a British penal colony. The point is named for Woollarawarre Bennelong, a Wangal man who was kidnapped on Phillip's orders and later became a go-between for the Eora and the colonists. I photographed it at dusk, after dark and, less gracefully, two miles into a morning run.

Personal reflection

There are a million Australian postcards taken from roughly where I was standing, so I really felt like a tourist! At the same time, we had just started the Australian studies talk, so I had a lot to think about. To the British, the harbour was terra nullius: land belonging to no one in any way their law would recognise. To the First Nations people, it was Country, already full of law, names and obligations. Our lecture estimated that about 70 per cent of the Eora died in the smallpox epidemic that followed.

I had not expected to feel implicated. The eleven ships sailed from Portsmouth in May 1787, an hour or so along the coast from Brighton (where I’m from). In Britain the First Fleet is a footnote, and convicts are mostly a joke you’d often hear about Australians at the cricket. Standing where the ships landed, it is harder to treat this as somebody else's history. The people who declared the harbour empty were sent by my government.

What surprised me is how openly Australia argues about it. The 26th of January is a public holiday that some people celebrate as Australia Day and others mourn as Invasion Day, and the argument happens every year. Both of my other countries have a way out. Americans mark the Fourth of July, a revolution against Britain, which lets them be the underdogs of their own founding. Britain has no national day at all, and so never has to decide what the empire was. Australia has no such escape hatch: its national day marks the arrival of the empire. I think that makes the founding harder to romanticise here.

Stanford, where I study, sits on the land of the Muwekma Ohlone and says so at the start of its events. Britain has no such ritual, because most of the land we would need to acknowledge is overseas. In Sydney the older name, Tubowgule, is printed beside the newer one.

TopicBondi beach rescue

Surf reportBONDI21 Sept 2026

Description

Bondi is Australia's most famous kilometre of sand. Its name comes from an Aboriginal word usually translated as the sound of water breaking over rocks. The Bondi Surf Bathers' Life Saving Club, founded in 1907, claims to be the oldest in the world, and volunteer lifesavers in red and yellow still mark the safe swimming zone with flags. Our lecture listed the beach among Australia's everyday icons, beside the Hills Hoist and the democracy sausage, and noted that about 87 per cent of Australians live within 50 km of the coast. We took our surfboards there on a public bus!

Personal reflection

I am partly from Brighton, where we have a pebble beach, the sea is a murky brown and swimming is something people do for a dare. I had surfed before, but commuting to the beach by city bus was new. We carried surfboards on board and nobody blinked! The beach here is treated as public infrastructure. Nobody owns the sand, the lifesavers are volunteers and the showers are free.

The course taught us a lot about Australian values. The beach is where the "fair go" is super visible, you’ll see people of every possible background in wetsuits waiting for the same wave. The English seaside has a class system of its own: hired deckchairs, piers with turnstiles, beach huts that cost as much as a car. California, where I study, says in law that its coast is public, yet a short drive from Stanford a billionaire has spent more than a decade in court trying to keep people off Martins Beach.

TopicHawke's Lager: a prime minister on a beer can

Hill aboveBONDI21 Sept 2026
"Any boss who sacks anyone for not turning up today is a bum."Bob Hawke, PM · America's Cup, 1983

Description

This is a can of Hawke's Lager, photographed on the hill above Bondi. The face on it belongs to Bob Hawke, the trade union leader who was Prime Minister from 1983 to 1991. Hawke was famous for setting a world record for drinking a yard of ale as a student at Oxford, and for announcing on live television, the morning Australia won the America's Cup in 1983, that "any boss who sacks anyone for not turning up today is a bum." The brewery launched in 2017 with his blessing, and he gave his share to Landcare, the conservation movement he launched nationally as Prime Minister. Hence the line on the can: "Every sip gives back."

Personal reflection

I had heard about Bob Hawke before I came, which is the only reason I recognised the face on the can. He was a prime minister who also held a world record for drinking a yard of ale, and the record seems to be what people remember most fondly. He set it as a student at Oxford!

That fondness is what our lecture on national identity called the larrikin: affection for someone irreverent, anti-authoritarian and unimpressed by rank. The course talked about the type from the Eureka diggers through Ned Kelly. A leader can be powerful as long as he never acts as though he is better than you. Hawke’s face on a beer can says the prime minister was one of us, and that he would have found the can funny too.

I can't imagine it at home. In Britain, the people who govern us are usually of the highest social class, and we get our revenge by mocking them. In America, where I study, the president is treated with incredible formality. Australia is the first place I have been where the affection and the joke are the same thing. People call the prime minister by a nickname and mean it kindly.

TopicThe pub and the pitch

Friday nightSYDNEY18 Sept 2026

BETTING SLIP

Wagered, 2022–23
$244.3b
…through pokies
$191.1b
Melbourne Cup
1st Tue Nov

Description

Two photographs of Australian sport - the first is an Irish bar in central Sydney on a Friday night in September, with horse racing on the screens behind us. The second is a game of rugby on Rainbow Beach with some Australians we met in October. Our sport lecture described how British games were imported and remade in Australia, with rugby strongest in New South Wales and Queensland, and how gambling grew up beside them: Australians wagered about $244 billion in 2022–23, $191 billion of it through poker machines, the "pokies" found in pubs and clubs across most of the country. The Melbourne Cup, run on the first Tuesday of November, is still called "the race that stops a nation".

Personal reflection

The pub photo was taken the night we arrived in Sydney - it was an Irish bar, which was unexpected! Later on, we learned about the Irish who followed during the famine.

The betting is familiar as well. Every British high street has a bookmaker, and football shirts have carried gambling logos for years. In the United States gambling was traditionally a destination, Las Vegas, until the Supreme Court let the states legalise sports betting in 2018, and now the ads fill every broadcast. What differs here is the scale, with poker machines in the corner of an ordinary local.

We ended up in a game of rugby on the beach with some Australians. Cricket and rugby came out with the British empire, and beating the "mother country" at its own games became one of the ways the colonies imagined themselves as a nation. For one game, I was the mother country. What I noticed was how fast a ball turned strangers into mates.

TopicA garden and a snack pack: from poll tax to multiculturalism

Darling HarbourSYDNEY19 Sept 2026

Description

The Chinese Garden of Friendship is a walled garden in Darling Harbour, on the edge of Sydney's Chinatown, with pavilions and willows backed by glass towers. It opened in 1988, and the plaque at my feet says it "celebrates the sister-state relationship of China's Guangdong province and the New South Wales Government." Guangdong is also where most of the Chinese miners on the 1850s goldfields came from. They were the largest non-European group on the diggings, and the colonies answered with a £10 arrival tax and, after Federation, with the Immigration Restriction Act 1901 and its dictation test. The third photo is a halal snack pack, or HSP: kebab meat, chips and sauces in a box, a creation of Australia's kebab shops, being eaten by Ben Vu at Dunwich on Minjerribah.

Personal reflection

I ended up in the garden by accident after going for a jetlagged early morning walk during my first day in Sydney. It is quiet in a way the middle of a city of five million should not be!

Reading the plaque, "friendship" is doing a lot of work. The province whose artisans helped build this garden is the one whose miners were taxed on arrival, pushed around the goldfields and then written out of the new nation in 1901. Our migration lecture traced the long turn from White Australia, through assimilation and its "New Australians", to official multiculturalism in the 1970s. The garden shows that last stage.

Both of my other countries are involved in this story. The colonies that taxed the Chinese miners were British colonies, and Britain fought the Opium Wars against the miners' home country in the same period. California, where I study, had its own gold rush just before Australia's, its own Foreign Miners' Tax and then the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882. Chinese labourers built the railroad whose fortune built my university. All three places grew rich on Chinese labour, restricted Chinese migration and now put up gardens and plaques.

The halal snack pack is the other half of the picture. It was invented in Australia's kebab shops, got an Australian abbreviation and is eaten by everybody.

TopicThe Blue Mountains

Gundungurra & Dharug CountryKATOOMBA29–30 Sept

Description

The Blue Mountains rise about 100 km west of Sydney. They are the Country of the Gundungurra and Dharug peoples and, since 2000, part of the Greater Blue Mountains World Heritage Area, listed largely for the diversity of its eucalypts. The blue haze comes from fine droplets of eucalyptus oil scattering the light. We hiked the cliff tracks, found a waterfall with a swimming hole and watched the sun go down over the valley from Katoomba. In the Black Summer of 2019–20, fire burned through most of this World Heritage Area; nationally, our lecture put the toll at about 19 million hectares and 3 billion animals.

Personal reflection

The lookout was beautiful. It was untouched wilderness as far as I could see.

The lecture on Aboriginal Australia argued that this landscape was never untouched. Country is a relationship that joins land, ancestors, law and the duty to care for a place. For thousands of years that care included fire: cool, patchy, deliberate burning suited to the season and the terrain. Bill Gammage called the result "the biggest estate on earth". What looked like wilderness to the British was closer to a garden whose gardeners they were pushing out.

The British, of all people, should have recognised this. Brighton sits under the South Downs, which look natural but are nothing of the sort. The open chalk grassland exists because people have grazed sheep on it for thousands of years, and it turns to scrub when they stop. Everyone at home knows the countryside is made. The settlers could not see that the same was true here, because the people doing the making were not farmers they recognised.

California, where I study, made the American version of the mistake. Yosemite was celebrated as pristine after the Ahwahneechee had been driven out of it, a century of putting out every fire left the forests primed to burn, and "fire season" is now part of the calendar. Tribes such as the Karuk and Yurok are leading the return of cultural burning there, much as Indigenous rangers are here.

TopicHeron Island

Great Barrier ReefHERON IS.2–12 Oct 2026

Description

Heron Island is a coral cay at the southern end of the Great Barrier Reef, about 80 km off Gladstone and small enough to walk around in half an hour. It lies inside a national park and the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, a World Heritage Area since 1981, and it hosts the University of Queensland research station where I spent ten days. Turtles feed on the reef (one photobombed my snorkel selfie), rays cruise the shallows after dark, and the sky is dark enough to photograph the Milky Way on a phone. This is the Sea Country of the Bailai, Gurang, Gooreng Gooreng and Taribelang Bunda peoples.

Personal reflection

I spent ten days on Heron for a course, fieldwork and research, and I have never been anywhere that made conservation feel less abstract. It’s impossible to treat nature as scenery when you are standing in it up to your neck. The turtle behind me in this photo did not care that I was there, which I took as the highest possible compliment to the people who manage the place.

Our last lecture argued that conservation is a negotiation among ecological, cultural and economic values, not a line drawn around something pretty. The reef is the clearest case I can think of. It is zoned, monitored and loved, and it sits downstream of the national economy. Gladstone, where the boat to Heron leaves from, is one of the world's big coal and gas ports. Australia protects the reef locally while exporting the fuel that warms it globally, and the mass bleaching events of the past decade show which side is winning. A marine park boundary is no use against water temperature.

I can't feel superior about that. Britain began the age of coal, and the United States perfected it. What I did notice is a difference in who gets a say. American national parks were built on the idea of nature without people. On the reef, Traditional Owners are increasingly written into management as partners, through Indigenous rangers and Sea Country agreements. That is the course's idea of Country turned into policy. It came late and it is incomplete, but it treats knowledge that is thousands of years old as an asset.

The Milky Way photo is here for scale. Heron made me feel small both when I was underwater and when I was looking up at night.

TopicK'gari

Butchulla CountryK'GARI16–17 Oct 2026
Butchulla lore
  1. What is good for the land comes first.
  2. Do not take or touch anything that does not belong to you.
  3. If you have plenty, you must share.

Description

K'gari is the world's largest sand island, with rainforest growing straight out of the dunes. It is Butchulla Country, and in the Butchulla language the name means paradise. For more than a century the maps said Fraser Island, after Captain James Fraser and his wife Eliza, who were shipwrecked here in 1836. The Federal Court recognised the Butchulla people's native title in 2014, and in 2023 the Queensland Government officially restored the name K'gari. The island's dingoes, called wongari, are protected, and visitors are told to keep their distance and carry a stick. My classmate is holding ours. The second photo is our Welcome to Country, the ceremony in which Traditional Owners welcome visitors onto their land.

Personal reflection

We saw wongari on K'gari, carried a dingo stick in case one came too close, and heard about the island from Butchulla people who work on it. They call it K'gari. Fraser Island, the name on the older maps and half the internet, sounded strange by the time we left. Funnily enough, my mum came here 30 years ago on her gap year before starting University.

We were also welcomed. An Acknowledgement of Country can be said by anyone, and opens most lectures here. A Welcome to Country can only be given by Traditional Owners, on their own Country. Ours came with a fire, boomerangs clapped together for rhythm, and clapsticks handed round so that we all joined in.

Terra nullius worked by making existing relationships to land invisible, and naming was one of its tools: call a place after a shipwrecked British couple and its much older history drops off the map. Mabo overturned the legal fiction in 1992, the Native Title Act followed in 1993, and the Butchulla won their determination in 2014. Restoring a name does not hand back an island, and native title is a weaker right than ownership. But it changes what every visitor says out loud.

Both of my other countries are having this argument. In Wales, the national park dropped "Snowdon" and "Snowdonia" in 2022 in favour of Yr Wyddfa and Eryri, the names Welsh speakers never stopped using. In Alaska, the mountain named for President McKinley, who never saw it, became Denali again in 2015, and in 2025 a new administration ordered it changed back. A restored name is not the finish line. It lasts only as long as the politics that restored it. Australians voted No to the Voice in 2023 and Queensland repealed its Path to Treaty the following year, so the ground is not settled here either.

TopicMinjerribah

Quandamooka CountryMINJERRIBAH6 Nov 2026
the people
the sun
the earth
HAROLD THOMAS, 1971

Description

Minjerribah, also known as North Stradbroke Island or "Straddie", is Quandamooka Country in Moreton Bay, a ferry ride from Brisbane. In 2011 the Federal Court recognised the Quandamooka people's native title over much of the island, and sand mining, which had run here for decades, ended in 2019. Near Point Lookout I found a roadside pole painted as the Aboriginal flag, designed by Luritja artist Harold Thomas in 1971: black for the people, red for the earth and a yellow sun. On this one, somebody has painted a dollar sign inside the sun. A minute down the road, we found another sign warning drivers about koalas and kangaroos.

Personal reflection

I can't tell you what the dollar sign means, but I have three theories. It could be a protest about the island, which was mined for its sand for decades while most of the money left. It could be about the flag itself, whose copyright was privately held until the Commonwealth paid about $20 million in 2022 so that anyone could fly it. Or it could be aimed at people like me, visitors who treat Aboriginal culture as something to buy on the way to the beach. All three would be fair.

Whichever it is, the pole brought the reconciliation lecture to life. The course described five dimensions of reconciliation, among them historical acceptance and institutional integrity, and stressed that it is the opposite of assimilation: a relationship between peoples who remain distinct. My other coursework this quarter kept bringing me back to Quandamooka Sea Country, and what struck me was how ordinary Indigenous presence is in Australian public life. The Aboriginal flag is an official flag of Australia, flown beside a national flag that still has my country's in its corner. Lectures begin with an Acknowledgement of Country. Neither Britain nor the United States has much of that visibility, and at first I thought Australia was simply ahead.

It is more tangled than that. Britain signed the Treaty of Waitangi with Māori in New Zealand in 1840, but never signed one here, because terra nullius meant there was officially nobody to sign with. The United States signed hundreds of treaties with Native nations, broke most of them, and still recognises tribes as sovereign governments. Australia has never concluded a national treaty.

TopicLamington

Yugambeh CountryLAMINGTON N.P.26–28 Oct 2026

Description

Lamington National Park sits on the rim of an ancient volcano on the Queensland–New South Wales border, in Yugambeh Country. Protected since 1915, it is part of the Gondwana Rainforests of Australia World Heritage Area, whose Antarctic beech trees descend from forests that grew when Australia was still joined to Antarctica. The first photo is the view from the escarpment. The second is a leech being removed from my friend Jack's ankle with a travel money card, which is the approved method: slide something flat under the sucker, and don't panic!

Personal reflection

The British mostly know Australian wildlife as a list of things that can kill you!

Our identity lecture described the bush legend: the tough, practical, dry-humoured bushman of Henry Lawson and Banjo Paterson, later canonised by Russel Ward, who survives a hostile land with his mates. Watching Stanford students deal with one leech, I understood both the legend's appeal and its exaggeration. Nobody was in danger, but overcoming challenges brought us together. In this case, one person held still and another operated!

Each of my three countries has a version of this story. Britain's is the stiff upper lip: endure it and say nothing. America's is the frontier and the cowboy: a rugged individual conquers the wilderness, alone, and gets rich. The Australian bushman endures it too, but with friends, and makes jokes along the way.

All three legends leave out the same people. This forest was Yugambeh Country long before it was a national park or a proving ground for bushmen, and the lecture's point was that the legend had no room for First Nations knowledge or for women's work. Most Australians also live in suburbs near the coast and meet the bush as we did: on a weekend, on a marked track, with snacks. I don't think that makes the legend false. It is a story a country tells about itself, as mine does, and I liked it better for the leech.
