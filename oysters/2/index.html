Return
the Shell

An oyster reef is built on the shells of the oysters that came before. On Quandamooka Sea Country, one way of living with oysters kept the shell coming back. Another took it away.

Scroll down to follow one oyster.

We acknowledge the Quandamooka People, Traditional Owners of the lands and waters of Quandamooka (Moreton Bay), including Minjerribah (North Stradbroke Island) and Mulgumpin (Moreton Island). We pay our respects to Elders past and present.

We are university students, not Quandamooka people, and we don't speak for Traditional Owners. What this story says about Quandamooka knowledge comes from published sources and from what Quandamooka people have chosen to share publicly. Every source is listed at the end.

A shallow bay behind islands of sand

Quandamooka is the bay between the mainland and the great sand islands of Minjerribah and Mulgumpin. Rivers bring fresh water and sediment in from the west. The islands hold back the ocean swell from the east.1

Sheltered, shallow and fed by rivers, it was once thick with oysters.1

One oyster

How a reef begins.

It starts adrift

A Sydney rock oyster (Saccostrea glomerata) begins life as a free-swimming larva, barely visible to the eye. When the water warms, millions are released at once and ride the tides of the bay for a few weeks.1

It needs something hard to hold

To survive, the larva has to cement itself to a hard surface. Once it does, it is called spat, and it never moves again.

The best surface of all is the shell of an older oyster.1

A reef is oysters on oysters

Oyster reefs form the way coral reefs do. As older animals die, new ones grow on what they leave behind. Layer on layer, the shell becomes a three-dimensional structure that shelters young fish, crabs and other shellfish.1

Take the shell away and the next generation has nowhere to land.

What a reef does for the bay

Oysters feed by pumping water over their gills and straining out algae and silt. A reef full of them keeps the water clear, and clear water lets sunlight reach the seagrass.1,2

About this story

This was made by students in the Coastal Ecosystems course at the University of Queensland as a science communication project. We are not Quandamooka people. We have tried to describe, as researchers, what published sources say about Quandamooka care for oysters alongside what Western science has learned, and we have not spoken to or for Traditional Owners.

The tidal-flat scene is an illustration, not a picture of a real place. It shows no Quandamooka people and uses no Quandamooka designs or imagery.

If we have got something wrong, we want to fix it.
