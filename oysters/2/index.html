Return
the Shell

An oyster reef is built on the shells of the oysters that came before. In Quandamooka, some people chose a way of living with oysters that kept the shell coming back, while another took it away. This story sets the two side by side and asks what that means for rebuilding the reefs now.

Below is the life of one oyster.

We acknowledge the Quandamooka People, who are the Traditional Owners of the lands and waters of Quandamooka (Moreton Bay). We pay our respects to Elders past and present.

We are university students and we don't speak for Traditional Owners.

A shallow bay behind islands of sand

Quandamooka is the shallow, warm estuarine bay that lies in between the mainland and the large sand islands of Minjerribah and Moreton. Large river catchments like the Brisbane, Pine Rivers, and many others, bring sediment and nutrients from inland. The islands hold back the ocean swell from the east.1

These warm, sheltered, and nutrient-high conditions meant this bay was once thick with oysters.1

One oyster

Early life: at the mercy of the ocean

A Sydney rock oyster (Saccostrea glomerata) begins life as a free-swimming larva that is smaller than a grain of sand. When the water is warm enough, millions are released at once. A single mother can release up to 25 million at a time. Because the larvae are zooplankton, they must ride the tides of the bay for a few weeks.1

Eventually, the larva must find something hard to hold

To survive, the larva has to cement itself to a hard surface. It grows a foot and eye spot to crawl and test surfaces. Once it does, it is called an oyster spat, and it never moves again.

The best surface of all is the shell of an older oyster, which is why oyster reefs form.1

A reef is oysters upon oysters

Oyster reefs form in a similar way to coral reefs. As older animals die, new ones grow on what they leave behind. Layer on layer, the shell becomes a three-dimensional structure that shelters young fish, crabs and other shellfish.1

Take the shell away and the next generation has nowhere to land.

What a reef does for the bay

Oysters feed by pumping water over their gills and straining out algae and silt. A reef full of them keeps the water clear, and clear water lets sunlight reach the seagrass, resulting in a positive cycle that contributes to a healthier ocean.1,2

One reef, two ways

The first way: the shell goes back

Quandamooka people have eaten oysters from this bay for a very long time. The shell middens around its shores are the record of it.1

Historian Jodi Frawley describes how the Dandrabin-Gorenpul peoples kept oysters plentiful: they watched the reefs closely, moved young oysters to where they would grow better, and put spent shell back in the water for the next generation to settle on.1

Remember what a larva needs. Returning shell is not a ritual added on top of harvesting. It is the step that makes the next harvest possible.

The second way: the shell goes away

From the 1860s, colonial oysterers worked the same reefs to supply growing towns. They broke reefs apart with steel spikes and dredges and worked each one for about three years, until every animal was gone. They called it “skinning” the reef, and expected it to grow back.1

The shell did not return to the water. It left with the oysters, and reef shell and midden shell alike were burned for lime to make mortar.3

By 1898 the fishery had collapsed across most of the bay.1

Going forward

Science has arrived at the same method

Reef restoration today works by putting old oyster shell back in the water, in baskets and reef modules, so that larvae have something to settle on. It is the first way, rediscovered.

Borrowing the method is the easy part. The comparison above is about more than technique: it is about who is responsible for a reef, and for how long.

Since 2011 the Quandamooka People's native title over land and waters around Minjerribah has been recognised in law, and reef trials on Quandamooka Sea Country are being run with Traditional Owner organisations.6,7

What Traditional Owners have asked for

In 2016, 21 Traditional Owners from around Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand, including Quandamooka people, met on Bribie Island at the north of this bay. They gave reef scientists seven pieces of advice:5

  1. Respect. Acknowledge Country, history, and Indigenous peoples' rights and responsibilities.
  2. Involve. Design and manage projects together.
  3. Think local. Build in local needs and Indigenous jobs.
  4. Share knowledge. Map what was there and what has changed, with the right people.
  5. Visualise. Agree on what a restored reef looks like. Avoid assigning blame.
  6. Engage. Start early and stay for the long term.
  7. Connect. Treat land and sea as one system.

Why the last one matters here

Dredging was only half of what killed the bay's reefs. The other half came from the land: cleared catchments sent mud down the rivers, and after the floods of the 1890s the surviving oysters were smothered and infested with mudworm.3

A reef project that only looks at the seabed repeats the old mistake of treating the reef as a thing on its own. “Recognise connections between land and sea” is advice from Traditional Owners, and it is also what the ecology says.5

Whose reef is it for?

Quandamooka artist Megan Cope has been planting a living artwork on Minjerribah: timber poles from the island, hung with oyster shell, for a reef to grow on.2

“It's actually not for us; it's for Country, it's for the oysters.”
Megan Cope, speaking to ABC News, 20222

That is the difference in one line. The second way asked what a reef could give. The first asks what it needs.

What you can do

  • When you hear about a reef restoration project, ask who is leading it, and whether Traditional Owners helped design it from the start.
  • Ask what happens to the shell when you eat oysters. Some venues send theirs back to the reef.
  • Learn from Quandamooka people directly, on a tour or at a cultural centre on Minjerribah.
  • Leave middens where they are. They are protected cultural heritage, and the oldest record of how to do this well.

A reef lasts when the shell goes back. That was known here long before anyone measured it.
