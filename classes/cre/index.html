CRE 1 / 2 October

Dangerous Marine Creatures

The central prevention rule is to avoid contact: wear suitable protective equipment, move slowly, do not touch or chase animals, and avoid nests or territories. A venomous animal delivers a toxin through a bite, sting, or spine; a mechanical injury can be serious even without venom.

Hazards and mechanisms

  • Cnidarian stings: corals, fire corals, hydroids, anemones, and jellyfish possess stinging structures. A cnidocyte is the cell; a nematocyst is the capsule inside it that discharges. The notes call nematocysts cells, but this distinction is more precise. Symptoms described include burning pain, itching, rash within about 30 minutes, and blistering in severe cases.
  • Coral cuts: sharp skeleton damages skin. Contamination, including organisms associated with coral mucus, can lead to infection. Swelling, tenderness, worsening redness, and sores require attention; do not treat the notes as a diagnosis guide.
  • Sea urchins: brittle spines can break in skin. Injury may be mechanical; some species also have venomous structures. Allergic reactions are possible.
  • Bristle worms: detachable bristles can penetrate skin; some carry irritating or venomous substances.
  • Sea snakes: venomous bites are a risk. Usually avoiding provocation reduces risk, but apparently calm behaviour does not make handling safe.
  • Stingrays: the tail spine can cause a deep puncture or laceration, with envenomation and tissue damage.
  • Lionfish and stonefish: venomous spines can cause severe pain. Stonefish camouflage makes accidental contact particularly dangerous. Triggerfish are a separate territorial/bite hazard; do not memorise them as having lionfish-type venomous spines.
  • Cone snails: a modified, dart-like tooth delivers venom. An attractive shell is not evidence that it is safe to pick up.
  • Blue-ringed octopuses: potentially lethal venom; the notes name the hazard but do not give a mechanism or first-aid protocol.
  • Sharks: bite/trauma hazard; the notes name them without detailed species-specific content.

Important correction: jellyfish treatment is species-specific

The PDF's "less vinegar and plenty of warm water" wording for box jellyfish is unsafe to learn as a general rule. ANZCOR guidance distinguishes potentially lethal jellyfish stings from bluebottles: vinegar is considered for potentially lethal species, not bluebottles; hot-water treatment is used for bluebottle pain. Suspected serious envenomation requires urgent emergency care. This is exam clarification, not a substitute for trained first aid or emergency advice.

Why can a coral cut be a problem even if it is not venomous?

The skeleton creates an open wound, and contamination can produce infection. Mechanical injury and infection are different mechanisms from venom injection.

CRE 2 / 3 October

Coral Biology

Written revision / inferred

Explain the animal, symbionts and skeleton as separate components. Be able to connect photosynthesis and feeding to calcification, then explain why bleaching is not automatically death. Distinguish sexual reproduction, budding and fragmentation.

A reef-building coral is an animal, not a plant or a rock. The polyp is the living animal; its corallite is the hard skeletal cup it secretes. Many polyps form a colony. Photosynthetic algae live in the animal's tissue, and the colony builds an aragonite calcium-carbonate skeleton.

The skeletal vocabulary

  • Polyp: the animal, including mouth, tentacles, and digestive cavity.
  • Corallite: the skeleton of one polyp; the notes give a size range from millimetres to centimetres.
  • Calice: the upper, cup-like region of a corallite, bounded by the wall.
  • Corallum: the skeleton of the whole colony. The PDF spells this "corralum".
  • Septum (plural septa): a radial skeletal plate projecting inward from a corallite wall.
  • Costae: radial skeletal ribs outside the wall, often continuing the septa.
  • Theca: the wall enclosing the calice.

Growth form is not the same as corallite arrangement

Growth form describes the colony's overall shape: foliose (leaf-like), branching, massive (solid mound), brain-like, plate/tabulate, encrusting (thin layer over substrate), hemispherical, or corymbose (branches forming a table-like cluster). Corallite arrangement describes how individual skeletal cups are organised:

  • Plocoid: separate corallites, each with its own wall.
  • Phaceloid: separate tubular corallites forming a branching/bundled colony; "uniform height" in the notes is not the defining feature.
  • Cerioid: adjacent corallites share walls; spelt "ceroid" in the PDF.
  • Meandroid: polyp mouths form valleys with shared walls.
  • Flabello-meandroid: valleys are separate rather than sharing common walls.
  • Dendroid: tree-like branching with distinct corallites.
  • Thamnasterioid: poorly defined corallite boundaries with confluent septa.

Do not identify species by colour alone. Symbionts, host pigments, condition, and lighting affect colour. Skeletal features and colony structure are more useful. Morphology also varies within a colony, across habitats, and between regions.

Species, geography, and hybrids

First, read the name

In Acropora palmata, Acropora is the genus, a group of related species; the complete two-word name identifies the species. A. palmata abbreviates the same name. A colony is one connected structure containing many polyps, not a species.

Geography and diversity

An assemblage is the set of species living in a place. Indo-Pacific and Caribbean reefs have different species pools, so location matters for identification. The Coral Triangle, around Indonesia, the Philippines and neighbouring countries, is an Indo-Pacific biodiversity hotspot: exceptionally species-rich, not exceptionally hot. Richness counts species; abundance counts individuals or colonies. Geographic context.

Different shapes do not necessarily mean different species

Morphology means physical form. Light, water movement and other conditions can change a colony's growth form; this is phenotypic plasticity. Pavona maldivensis has plate-like and columnar forms, sometimes within one colony. Historically, the forms received different names. Shape alone is therefore insufficient: compare skeletal features and, where available, genetic/reproductive evidence. Pavona example.

Hybrids: the Acropora example

A hybrid inherits genes from two different species. Caribbean A. prolifera comes from A. palmata (elkhorn) and A. cervicornis (staghorn): an egg from one is fertilised by sperm from the other.

F1 means first filial generation: direct offspring of that cross. In the 2002 study, sampled A. prolifera were F1 hybrids; their form differed according to which species supplied the egg. Different-looking hybrids can therefore share the same two parent species. Original study.

More colonies does not mean another hybrid generation

Fragmentation occurs when a broken piece survives and grows into a new colony. This is asexual propagation, not a new fertilisation. Those fragments are not F2 offspring: F2 requires sexual reproduction between F1 individuals.

Asexual spread does not prove sterility. A. prolifera can also reproduce sexually and backcross (breed with a parental species), although natural backcrossing appears limited. Learn the study's F1 finding, not the incorrect rule that all hybrids are sterile or automatically new species. NOAA assessment.

Do two different coral shapes establish two species?

No. Pavona can produce different forms within one species. Compare diagnostic skeletal features and genetic/reproductive evidence rather than shape alone.

Ten fragments grow from one F1 hybrid. Are these ten new crosses?

No: ten additional colonies propagated asexually from the existing hybrid, not ten fertilisation events or an F2 generation.

Where the coral's energy comes from

A coral contains two partners: the animal host and microscopic photosynthetic organisms living inside its tissue. Zooxanthellae is the traditional name for these symbiotic dinoflagellates; Symbiodiniaceae is the family name commonly used now. They live inside gastrodermal cells, in the tissue lining the animal's digestive cavity.

  1. The symbiont captures light. Photosynthesis uses light energy to turn inorganic carbon, such as CO2, into organic compounds. Organic compounds store chemical energy.
  2. Some products move to the host. These can include compounds such as glycerol. The animal uses transferred carbon compounds for metabolism or stores carbon/energy in forms such as fats. The coral is receiving food made by another organism; its animal cells do not themselves become photosynthetic.
  3. The animal supports the symbiont. Its tissue provides habitat, while metabolic processes supply compounds that can be recycled, including CO2 and inorganic nitrogen nutrients.
  4. The partnership supports coral functions. Available energy helps fund tissue maintenance, growth, reproduction, defence and skeleton building.

Both partners benefit, so the relationship is mutualism. It also creates a vulnerability: losing symbionts removes a major energy input. The coral may continue feeding on prey, but that does not guarantee it can replace all the lost photosynthetic supply.

Heterotrophy means obtaining energy by consuming organic food. Coral tentacles and nematocysts capture prey. This can partly support bleached corals, but capacity varies by species and growth form. Stable-isotope analysis can help infer food sources because different sources leave different chemical signatures.

Calcification

The simplified reaction is:

Ca2+ + CO32− → CaCO3

Ca2+ is a calcium ion; CO32− is a carbonate ion; CaCO3 is calcium carbonate. Coral skeleton is the mineral form aragonite. This is a simplified description, not the full chemistry of the calcifying fluid.

Corals regulate conditions, including pH, at the calcification site. That gives some control but does not make them immune to acidification or dissolution. The notes report declining calcification and suppression lasting up to four years after some bleaching events.

Measuring growth

  • Staining and sampling: mark a growth boundary, then inspect new skeleton after growth. Removing samples can be destructive.
  • Buoyant weight: estimate skeletal mass from weight while submerged. Unlike the PDF's grouping, this need not be destructive.
  • Tagging: revisit the same colonies to measure change; less destructive but measurement method still matters.
  • Photogrammetry / structure from motion (SfM): reconstruct three-dimensional shape from overlapping photographs; compare area, volume, or structure over time without removing the coral.

Reproduction: two routes, two stages

Asexual reproduction creates genetically related polyps through budding, or new colonies through fragmentation. Intratentacular budding occurs within the tentacle ring; extratentacular budding occurs outside it. "Extracellular" in the notes is likely a transcription error.

Sexual reproduction: many corals are hermaphrodites (produce eggs and sperm), while some have separate sexes. Broadcast spawners release gametes into the water for fertilisation. Environmental cues can include lunar, seasonal, and temperature signals; timing varies, so "full moon = reduced currents" is not a general rule. Brooders retain early development and release larvae; the notes describe larvae already containing symbionts.

A planula is a larval coral. It disperses, becomes competent to settle, attaches to suitable substrate, and metamorphoses into a juvenile polyp. Settlement is a high-mortality bottleneck. The notes give a larval time of less than three weeks in the case described, but durations and spawning seasons differ among species.

Bleaching, resistance, and resilience

Bleaching is the loss of symbiotic algae and/or their pigments, revealing the pale skeleton through tissue. A bleached coral may still be alive. Prolonged energy shortage can cause mortality.

Resistance means suffering less damage during a stress event; resilience means recovering afterwards. More heat-tolerant symbionts may improve resistance but can carry growth trade-offs. The lecture uses old clade labels: D is often discussed as heat tolerant; some C subgroups also tolerate heat. Neither label guarantees the same response in every host.

A microbiome is the community of associated microorganisms. Microbiome transplantation introduces microbes from a donor to a recipient coral, aiming to transfer beneficial functions such as stress protection; it is an experimental intervention, not guaranteed recovery. They also record five GBR mass-bleaching events in eight years ending in 2024: keep that as historical lecture context, not a count through today.

Downstream chain: bleaching → possible coral death → reduced living cover and altered colony structure → erosion/lower rugosity → changed fish habitat and populations → broader ecosystem effects. Rugosity means surface complexity/roughness, not simply coral cover. Dead skeleton can initially retain structure before it erodes.

Why can a reef lose ecological function after coral mortality even if the skeleton remains?

Living tissue and some food relationships are lost immediately. Later, skeleton breaks down and habitat complexity declines. Fish that need living coral and fish that need shelter may therefore respond on different time scales.

Coral Reef Fish

Written revision / inferred

Recognise distinguishing anatomical features and functional groups, not just colours. Connect herbivore identity and size to grazing/bioerosion. The full lecture identification list is still missing; this page cannot certify complete identification coverage.

Fish shape and anatomy help explain how they swim, feed and use reef habitat. An identification answer needs a diagnostic feature, not just a colour. The lecture lists two major groups: bony fish (Osteichthyes) and cartilaginous fish (Chondrichthyes, including sharks and rays).

Body shape tells you something about lifestyle

  • Fusiform: streamlined/spindle-shaped; useful for efficient sustained swimming.
  • Laterally compressed: thin side-to-side; common in manoeuvrable reef fish.
  • Depressed: flattened top-to-bottom; useful for bottom-associated life, as in rays.
  • Anguilliform: eel-like; useful for moving through crevices.
  • Globiform: rounded; not designed for fast sustained swimming.

Mouth position and shape help indicate how food is collected; fins affect propulsion, steering, and stability. Record whether a fish is benthic (bottom-associated), mid-water, or pelagic (open-water), and whether it schools, pairs, lives alone, or forms a symbiosis.

Operculum: the bony gill cover. The preoperculum lies just in front of it; its spine distinguishes angelfish from butterflyfish. The lateral line detects water movement. Caudal means tail fin; pectoral fins are the paired fins behind the gills. Dorsal (back) and anal (underside) fins help stabilise the fish.

Identification and ecological roles

  • Damselfish (Pomacentridae): one nostril on each side, interrupted lateral line, often a forked tail. Includes clownfish/anemonefish. Diets include algae, plankton, and mixed foods; many are territorial. The notes say most are small, largest about 30 cm, common in shallow tropical reefs.
  • Butterflyfish (Chaetodontidae): laterally compressed, disk-like body; small, sometimes elongated mouth; prominent spiny dorsal fin; rounded caudal fin in the notes. False eyespots or an eye-crossing stripe can confuse predators. Benthic diets vary, including coral tissue and invertebrates.
  • Angelfish (Pomacanthidae): the key distinction from butterflyfish is a strong preopercular spine, on the gill-cover region. Continuous dorsal fin, rounded tail, often striking juvenile-to-adult pattern changes. Brightness alone is not diagnostic.
  • Groupers / rock cods: predatory reef fish. Coral trout are colourful, medium-sized examples; Queensland groupers can be very large. The notes do not provide a full diagnostic key.
  • Wrasses (Labridae): often prominent canine teeth and pectoral-fin swimming. Cleaner wrasses remove ectoparasites (parasites on the outside of a host) at cleaning stations: the cleaner gets food and the client loses parasites. Humphead/Maori/Napoleon wrasse names refer to the large humphead wrasse example; facial lines help identification. Not every wrasse is a cleaner.
  • Moray eels: often shelter in holes; strong smell compensates for limited vision. The notes list cephalopods, molluscs, and crustaceans as prey, and groupers, barracudas, and sea snakes as predators.
  • Parrotfish: fused beak-like teeth; often a crescent tail; pectoral fins for normal swimming, tail for bursts. Scraping removes algae and sometimes carbonate, producing sand. Many species change sex from female to male. Grazing can maintain settlement space for corals; scraping can also contribute to bioerosion.
  • Snappers: common food fish, often schooling; slightly upturned snout in the lecture key. Can be confused with emperors. Do not assume the snout alone resolves every species.
  • Sweetlips and grunts: thick lips, low-set mouths, rounder heads than the snapper example.
  • Triggerfish: locking/trigger-like first dorsal spine; often solitary daytime carnivores; use dorsal and anal fin undulation for propulsion.
  • Porcupinefish: inflate and raise spines; broad fins and relatively large eyes compared with pufferfish in the notes; strong teeth crush hard prey such as urchins and crustaceans.
  • Lionfish and stonefish: venomous-spine hazards. Lionfish have conspicuous fin rays; stonefish are strongly camouflaged.
  • Tangs, rays, and sharks: named in the lecture notes but not given detailed identification descriptions.

Why survey fish?

Surveys estimate abundance, diversity, size structure, biomass, or functional roles. Those inform ecological condition and fisheries/economic decisions. BRUV means baited remote underwater video. It samples without a diver at the camera, but bait changes which species are attracted; it is not a neutral census of every fish. AI may assist annotation, but results still need validation.

Coverage gap: the notes say "26 families, 28 species" but do not supply that complete list or all species images. This guide cannot reconstruct a complete identification exam key from those numbers. The missing lecture key is especially important for a field book or oral identification exam.

How can parrotfish both help corals and erode reefs?

Removing algae can free settlement space and reduce competition. Scraping or excavating the substrate also removes carbonate. The net ecosystem effect depends on species, size, feeding behaviour, and the condition of the reef.

Reef Invertebrates

Written revision / inferred

Compare body plans and feeding mechanisms: sponge filtration, cnidarian stinging, molluscan body parts, echinoderm tube feet/test, arthropod joints/moulting. Know why fan worms and tunicates do not belong with arthropods.

An invertebrate lacks a vertebral column. It is not one taxonomic group: reef invertebrates belong to many phyla. A phylum is a broad classification within a kingdom, not the largest level of animal classification.

Sponges / Porifera

Sponges lack the true tissues and organs found in many other animals. Coordinated cell activities carry out feeding and transport. Choanocytes (collar cells) move water and capture food; water enters through small pores and exits through larger openings called oscula. Filtering bacteria and other particles links sponges to reef nutrient cycling. Some boring sponges also erode carbonate.

Molluscs / Mollusca

The basic body plan includes head, muscular foot, visceral mass (organs), mantle (tissue that secretes a shell), and often a shell. Different groups modify or lose parts of that plan. A radula is a tooth-bearing feeding ribbon; it can scrape algae or be modified for specialised feeding. Bivalves do not have a radula.

  • Gastropods: snails and slugs, including grazers and predators. Moon snails typically drill prey shells; the PDF's venomous dart description fits cone snails, not moon snails.
  • Nudibranchs: sea slugs with specialised diets. Some retain defensive chemicals from prey; bright colours can warn predators. Some aeolid nudibranchs retain undischarged cnidarian nematocysts in their cerata, the projections on their backs.
  • Bivalves: two valves/shells. Many filter-feed using gills that also exchange gases. Giant clams are the large tropical exception noted in class. Do not generalise haemoglobin to every bivalve from the notes.
  • Cephalopods: squid, cuttlefish, octopuses; active predators on fish and crustaceans. Complex behaviour and colour changes aid signalling and camouflage. Brooding and life histories vary, rather than every cephalopod brooding in the same way.

Echinoderms / Echinodermata

Adults typically have five-part radial organisation; larvae are generally bilateral. A water vascular system operates tube feet for movement, attachment, and feeding. The five groups in the notes are sea urchins, sea cucumbers, sea stars, brittle stars, and crinoids (sea lilies/feather stars).

  • Sea urchins: a calcareous internal skeleton called a test; spines for defence and movement; tube feet for attachment. Aristotle's lantern is the jaw apparatus, generally beneath the animal. Herbivorous grazing can remove algae and erode substrate.
  • Sea cucumbers: many process sediment and organic matter; some suspension-feed. Some eject sticky defensive Cuvierian tubules; the PDF's "anal ejaculate" is not the anatomical term. Sediment processing recycles nutrients.
  • Sea stars: can regenerate arms; crown-of-thorns sea stars evert the stomach over coral and digest tissue externally. Their nervous system includes a nerve ring and radial nerves, not a vertebrate-like brain.
  • Brittle stars: slender, articulated arms used for rapid movement; distinct from the broad-arm sea-star body form.
  • Crinoids: feathery arms for suspension feeding.

Arthropods / Arthropoda

Jointed appendages and an external skeleton are defining features. Marine examples include shrimps, crabs, and lobsters. An exoskeleton cannot expand continuously, so growth requires moulting. Snapping shrimps use a specialised claw to generate a rapid, powerful snap involved in prey capture, defence, and communication.

Two groups easy to misplace

Fan worms are annelids, not arthropods, despite being nested under arthropods in the notes. Rapid withdrawal is enabled by fast neural signalling, including large nerve fibres.

Tunicates are chordates, despite being invertebrates. Their larvae have chordate features including a notochord and dorsal nerve cord, many of which are lost or modified in the sessile adult. "Chordate" does not mean "vertebrate".

Compare sponge filtration, urchin grazing, and sea-cucumber feeding.

A sponge filters particles from water; an urchin scrapes food from surfaces with its jaw apparatus; many sea cucumbers ingest/process sediment. These are different pathways through which organisms move energy and recycle material.

Reading an animal's body plan

Radial symmetry arranges body parts around a central axis; bilateral symmetry gives matching left and right sides. Tissue layers, skeleton, appendages and development also distinguish animal groups. Mesoderm is the middle embryonic tissue layer. A coelom is a fluid-filled body cavity lined by mesoderm. It is not simply the digestive cavity. Cnidarians have two principal tissue layers; bilaterian animals have three embryonic germ layers. Sponges lack true tissues in the conventional comparison. These traits help distinguish major groups; colour alone does not.

More feeding and life-history examples

  • Giant triton: a predatory gastropod that eats crown-of-thorns sea stars. Predator protection is a possible ecological intervention, not proof that one predator alone controls outbreaks.
  • Crabs: jointed limbs, a compact body with the abdomen tucked beneath it, internal fertilisation, brooding of eggs, and larval release followed by settlement. Moulting enables growth. The deck describes experiments testing more than 100 predator species against juvenile crown-of-thorns: juvenile survival depends on predation as well as adult ecology.
  • Snapping shrimp: rapid claw closure produces a jet and cavitation bubble. Bubble collapse produces the characteristic sound; it is not merely two claw surfaces striking each other.
  • Polychaete annelids: segmented worms, often hidden in tubes or substrate. They do not have arthropod jointed legs. Fan-worm withdrawal illustrates a rapid nervous response.
  • Ctenophores: comb jellies form a separate animal group from cnidarians. Their rows of beating cilia are not jellyfish tentacles, and evolutionary placement is an active research question.

Coral DNA damage and variation within a colony

UV exposure can damage DNA. Excision repair removes a damaged section and replaces it; photoreactivation uses light-dependent enzymes to reverse particular damage. The deck cites Reef et al. (2009) for a large light-dependent contribution to repair in the studied corals, not a universal percentage for every species. Keep light-sensitive signalling proteins, such as cryptochromes, separate from the DNA-repair role of photolyases.

A somatic mutation arises in body cells; a germline mutation can pass through reproductive cells. Different branches of a coral can accumulate variation. The lecture raises the possibility that some within-colony mutations enter gametes: it does not mean every mutation in every branch is inherited.

Four reef configurations

  • Fringing: reef close to a shoreline.
  • Barrier: reef separated from the shore by a substantial lagoon.
  • Patch: an isolated reef patch, often within a lagoon.
  • Atoll: a reef ring around a lagoon. The classic model links reef growth to subsidence of an underlying volcanic island; growth, erosion, and sea-level history all matter.

Earth Observation and Reef Maps

Written revision + notebook link

Explain why a satellite pixel is not automatically a habitat label. Compare resolution/coverage, active/passive sensing, training/independent validation, and point-intercept/photo-quadrat sampling. These method comparisons also appear in the notebook tasks.

A habitat map records what is where. It helps scientists locate reef habitats, design sampling, compare conditions, and manage use. A satellite image is not already a coral-cover map: light must be measured, corrected, interpreted, and checked against reality.

Spatial vocabulary

  • GIS: geographic information system; stores, analyses, and combines information with known locations.
  • Raster: a grid of cells/pixels, each with a value. A satellite image and a depth grid are examples.
  • Vector: points, lines, or polygons. Survey locations are points; transects are lines; habitat boundaries can be polygons. Vector is not only polygons.
  • Georeferencing: attaching real-world coordinates to an image/object so it aligns with other spatial data.
  • Datum: a reference framework for Earth's shape and positions; "origin" alone is incomplete.
  • Coordinate system: the system used to express position, such as latitude/longitude or a projected grid.
  • Projection: mathematical conversion from curved Earth to a flat map; it introduces distortion. Layers using incompatible references can appear displaced.

What the sensor actually sees

The recorded signal combines the atmosphere, sea surface, water column, and seabed. Reflection/scattering redirects light; absorption removes it from the measured path; transmission allows it to pass through; fluorescence re-emits light at a different wavelength. Tides, waves, suspended sediment, and dissolved/organic material alter the signal.

Depth matters: light attenuates on the way down and back up. Red light is absorbed more quickly than blue/green. Blue/green bands are therefore particularly useful for shallow-water mapping. The same bottom type may look different at different depths, and different habitats may look similar through turbid water.

Sun glint is surface reflection that can hide the bottom. Clouds obscure images; rough water changes reflection; a pixel can contain several bottom types. If the bottom is not visible, optical benthic classification is unreliable, although water properties may still be mapped.

Active versus passive

A passive sensor receives energy that is already present. A camera taking a sunlit reef photograph records light from the sun reflected through the water. It does not send out the sunlight, so clouds, shadows and time of day affect its illumination.

An active sensor sends out a signal and measures what returns. LiDAR emits laser pulses; sonar emits sound. The travel time can help determine distance or depth when signal speed and geometry are known. Active does not mean "automatically better": each signal has its own penetration, resolution and measurement limits. The distinction is who supplies the measured energy, not whether the instrument is powered on.

Resolution, extent, and information

The lecture gives indicative scales: diver observations about 1 mm, drone imagery about 5 cm, satellite pixels around 5-30 m. These are examples, not universal sensor limits. Spatial resolution is pixel/detail size; extent is area covered; spectral resolution describes wavelength bands. A 10 m pixel covers 100 m2, not 10 m2. Small colonies can be mixed into one satellite pixel.

Image to habitat map: the workflow

  1. Define the question and classes: for example sand, seagrass, coral-dominated substrate. Your classification scheme must match the management question.
  2. Acquire appropriate imagery: choose resolution, coverage, timing, water clarity, and suitable bands.
  3. Correct the data: radiometric correction addresses sensor/measurement effects; atmospheric correction addresses the atmosphere; geometric/georeferencing correction aligns locations. The PDF says further preprocessing steps are on unavailable slides.
  4. Gather training data: known field examples show the algorithm what each class looks like.
  5. Classify or model: pixel-based classification labels individual pixels; object-based classification groups coherent image objects; continuous models may estimate depth or other quantities instead of discrete classes.
  6. Validate independently: compare predictions with held-out field observations, not just the same examples used for training.

A spectral signature is the pattern of measured response across wavelengths. Supervised classification learns signatures from known examples, then applies them elsewhere. Combining fine drone data with broad satellite maps can add detail in priority areas. Coverage and accuracy trade off; neither more pixels nor a confident algorithm guarantees ecological accuracy.

Why can "dark pixel = coral" fail?

Depth, water clarity, shadow, surface conditions, and mixed substrates can also darken a pixel. Classification needs multiple bands, environmental corrections, and independent field validation.

Field Monitoring

Ecological sampling measures what lives on a reef, how much is present, or what processes occur. Monitoring repeats comparable measurements through time. The field book now supplies the practical methods absent from the lecture notes.

Match the variable to the method

  • Line-intercept transect (LIT): measure lengths over substrate categories; estimates proportional cover.
  • Point-intercept: classify what lies under fixed points; estimates cover from point counts.
  • Belt transect: count organisms in a defined area; estimates abundance/density and can record sizes.
  • Photo quadrat: photograph a standardised patch, then annotate categories; gives a permanent image record.
  • CoralWatch: score coral colours as a bleaching-related indicator; not a census of cover or species.
  • Chain and tape: compare contoured surface distance with straight distance; measures rugosity.
  • SfM: reconstruct 3D shape from overlapping photos; repeat to measure change.

Cover (%) = intercepted category length ÷ total transect length × 100. For point-intercept data, use category points ÷ total points × 100. Example: 3.2 m of a 10 m line over live hard coral gives 32%. This is cover, not colony count.

Monitoring, calibration, validation

Baseline records a reference condition. Monitoring tracks changes. Calibration fits a model to field data; validation tests predictions against independent data. Sampling only convenient healthy colonies biases conclusions. Repeat comparable effort, depth, habitat, timing, and methods, and retain metadata.

Replicates help describe variability. Observations next to one another may not be independent, and repeatedly photographing one coral does not create many independent coral colonies. Before/after measurements with suitable controls are stronger for impact attribution than one visit after a disturbance.

Why can 40 fish yesterday versus 60 today fail to show a population increase?

Area, effort, visibility, tide, time, fish movements, and detection can differ. Standardise the survey and replicate before attributing the difference to abundance change.

Marine Plants and Algae

Official oral link / blue carbon

For the blue-carbon oral topic, separate a carbon stock from annual sequestration. Explain seagrass sediment storage, benefits, monitoring and permanence; prepare a supported project case study, which is not fully supplied here. For written revision, distinguish turf, Halimeda, CCA and seagrass.

"Marine plants" here includes organisms with very different biology: microalgae/phytoplankton, macroalgae, seagrasses, and mangroves. Algae are not simply small flowering plants. Benthic means on/associated with the bottom; pelagic means in the water column.

Microalgae and plankton

Microalgae are microscopic photosynthetic organisms. Plankton are organisms carried predominantly by water movement; photosynthetic plankton are called phytoplankton. Diatoms and dinoflagellates are examples. Some dinoflagellates instead live as coral symbionts, so the group name does not mean every member floats freely in the water.

Cyanobacteria are photosynthetic bacteria, not algae with a cell nucleus. Some fix nitrogen: they turn nitrogen gas into compounds organisms can use to build proteins and other molecules. Photosynthesis makes organic carbon; nitrogen fixation supplies a usable nitrogen source. These are different processes.

Chlorophyll a is a light-absorbing pigment. Measuring it provides an indirect indicator of photosynthetic plankton biomass: more pigment can suggest more photosynthetic material, but does not count species or directly measure all production. High plankton biomass/productivity can occur in nutrient-rich polar waters while tropical reef communities remain highly species-rich. Biomass, production and diversity answer different questions.

Three macroalgal groups

  • Green algae / Chlorophyta: chlorophyll a and b. Unicellular and multicellular forms. Green lineages are related to the ancestry of land plants. Uses noted: wastewater treatment, biofuels, nutritional supplements.
  • Brown algae: chlorophyll a and c plus fucoxanthin, a brown pigment. The notes call them Phaeophyta. Mostly multicellular; a holdfast attaches them to substrate, while gas bladders in some forms lift tissue toward light. A holdfast is not a nutrient-absorbing root. Alginate is used as a thickener/stabiliser, including in foods and toothpaste.
  • Red algae / Rhodophyta: chlorophyll a and accessory pigments including phycoerythrin, which helps absorb available underwater light. Some occur at considerable depth. The notes also mention chlorophyll d; pigment details vary, so do not assume every red alga has the same pigment complement. Nori is an edible red-algal product.

Three Heron categories

Turf algae are short assemblages rather than one species. Halimeda is a calcifying green alga that contributes carbonate segments/sediment. CCA means crustose coralline algae: calcifying red algae that encrust surfaces, help bind reef framework, and can provide coral settlement cues. Not all algae have the same effect on reefs.

What controls algae?

Bottom-up control comes from resources/conditions such as light, nutrients, and carbon supply. Top-down control comes from consumers such as grazing fish and urchins. Fast algal growth after nutrient enrichment or reduced grazing can shade corals, occupy settlement space, and interfere with recruitment. Warmer conditions do not help every alga indefinitely.

A phase shift is a change in the dominant community state, for example coral-dominated to macroalgal-dominated. Recovery can depend on growth of surviving corals, restored herbivory, coral competitive ability, and seasonal seaweed decline. A phase shift is not necessarily instantaneous or permanently irreversible.

Seagrasses and blue carbon

Seagrasses are marine angiosperms: flowering plants with true roots and vascular transport tissue. Meadows store organic carbon in biomass and sediments, helping create blue-carbon stocks. Carbon stock is an amount stored; sequestration rate is storage per time. They are not interchangeable.

The deck reports 0.1% of ocean area, 18% of carbon storage, and "40 times" forest-soil capture efficiency. These comparisons lack sufficiently explicit denominators/time baselines to use as universal rates. The 140 Mg/ha figure is better specified: organic carbon in the top metre of soil, accumulated over centuries to millennia. Mg means megagram, equivalent to a metric tonne. The stock/rate calculation below explains this distinction. Mangroves are named, but no detailed mangrove lecture content is supplied.

Does "more algae" always mean worse reef health?

No. CCA can bind framework and support settlement, and Halimeda produces carbonate sediment. Excess macroalgae can inhibit coral recovery. You need to identify the algal group, abundance, and ecological role.

Source: PDF pp. 31-33. Marine Plants and Ecology share lecture number 7 in the source.

Where Heron algae occur, and why timing matters

The deck's reef-slope examples include turf, Halimeda and CCA. The lagoon has greater macroalgal abundance and diversity in the illustrated comparison. "More diverse" means more kinds, not just more mass. Habitat, light, substrate, water movement and consumers vary across the reef, so a lagoon observation should not be extrapolated to the entire island.

Algae also changes seasonally. Compare surveys at similar seasons before interpreting a difference as long-term degradation. The lecture identifies thermal stress, cyclones, fishing, eutrophication and sediment disturbance as routes toward algal dominance. Recovery can involve coral remnants, competitive corals, seasonal seaweed decline and effective protection of ecological processes through MPAs.

Primary production is a rate

Primary production is conversion of inorganic carbon into organic material. The slides express production per surface area as g C m−2 day−1, or per water volume as g C m−3 day−1. The first is grams of carbon per square metre per day; the second is per cubic metre per day. Neither is a stored-carbon total.

The newly supplied slide specifies approximately 140 Mg organic C per hectare in the top metre of soil, accumulated over centennial to millennial timescales. That is 140 metric tonnes of carbon per hectare of the stated soil layer, not 140 tonnes newly captured each year. For a hypothetical 2 ha meadow at that density, the corresponding soil stock is 2 × 140 = 280 tonnes C. It excludes uncounted deeper soil unless the estimate explicitly includes it.

Why protect blue carbon, and what a carbon-credit claim needs

Seagrass traps particles, contributes organic matter and can bury carbon in sediment. Degradation can both stop further accumulation and expose stored carbon to loss. Mapping meadow area alone does not measure its full soil carbon: the deck's remote-sensing example uses observable proxies and field measurements.

Potential benefits include habitat protection, rehabilitation funding and retained carbon. Evaluating a project requires a credible baseline, evidence that the intervention adds a benefit beyond that baseline (additionality), durable storage (permanence), monitoring, costs and responsibility for future losses. These are explanatory criteria for assessing the deck's carbon-market proposal, not an assertion that every seagrass project meets them.

Ecological Principles

Written revision / inferred

Trace the mechanism, not just the vocabulary: grazers affect algae, algae affects recruitment, and grazing can also erode carbonate. Use the exclusion experiment to discuss causation and controls; use the fishing diagram to avoid an oversimplified trophic cascade.

A reef is a network of relationships. Explain the process and its consequence, not just the name: who benefits, who loses, and how does that change the community?

Why reefs matter

The lecture notes say reefs support about 25% of marine species in about 1% of ocean area and can absorb up to 97% of wave energy. The ecology deck describes support for hundreds of millions of people, broader than the student notes' 100 million figure. These are broad lecture estimates, not fixed values for every reef. Shoreline protection depends on reef shape, elevation, condition, and incoming waves.

The productive-reef / low-nutrient-water puzzle

Tropical surface waters often have low nutrient concentrations and low chlorophyll. Reef productivity is nevertheless supported by recycling, symbiosis, and other food pathways. Corals provide habitat and recycled inorganic nutrients; symbionts provide photosynthetic products. The notes' statement that corals "fertilise" algae with oxygen is misleading: photosynthesis uses inorganic carbon and releases oxygen. Corals and symbionts also exchange nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus.

Structure shelters small fish; algae feeds grazers; smaller animals support predators. Piscivores eat fish. A simple chain is useful, but real reefs have food webs with many overlapping diets.

Symbiosis and competition

Symbiosis is a close association between species; it does not automatically mean both benefit. Mutualism means both benefit, such as a cleaner wrasse obtaining food while reducing a client's ectoparasites.

Competition occurs when organisms use a limiting resource such as space or light. Corals may damage neighbours with sweeper tentacles or extruded mesenterial filaments. Competitive exclusion predicts that species competing identically for the same limiting resource cannot coexist indefinitely under constant conditions. Reef heterogeneity, disturbance, predation, and different resource use complicate that simplified outcome.

Disturbance and succession

Disturbance removes organisms or changes resources/habitat. Succession is the subsequent change in community composition. The intermediate disturbance hypothesis proposes high diversity at intermediate disturbance: too little can allow dominant competitors to exclude others; too much can prevent many species establishing. It is a hypothesis with context-dependent support, not a law that more disturbance always improves diversity.

Herbivory and feedbacks: Jamaica

In the 1980s Jamaican example, loss of Diadema urchins reduced grazing. Algae expanded, helping inhibit coral recruitment. The mechanism matters: losing a grazer changes competitive conditions for coral, even though the grazer is not a coral.

A positive feedback amplifies change; "positive" does not mean beneficial. For example, more algae may hinder coral recovery and sustain an algal state. A negative feedback counteracts change. The PDF's "positive feedback loops are vital for stabilisation" wording conflates amplification and stabilisation.

Trophic cascades and fishing

A trophic cascade occurs when a change at one feeding level indirectly changes others. Removing predators can release their prey; removing herbivores can release algae. Whether algae rises or falls depends on who is actually removed and the food-web structure.

The notes contrast spearfishing, often aimed at predatory fish but also parrotfish, with nets/traps that may heavily affect herbivores. Do not memorise "fishing increases herbivores": direct removal and indirect predator-release effects can oppose one another.

Marine protected areas (MPAs) restrict activities to protect ecological processes. Their effects depend on rules, enforcement, placement, connectivity, and pressures outside boundaries. The more complex food-web diagram from the supplied lecture is unpacked below.

Why might predator removal and herbivore removal have different effects on coral?

Predator removal can indirectly alter grazer populations, while herbivore removal directly reduces grazing. Less grazing can allow algae to occupy coral settlement space. The outcome depends on diets and which fish are targeted, so trace the actual links.

Source: PDF pp. 33-34.

Foundation species versus ecosystem engineers

A foundation species has a major role in structuring a community, often by providing abundant habitat. An ecosystem engineer changes the physical environment and thereby changes conditions for other organisms. Reef-building corals can be both: they dominate habitat and build the framework. The deck also uses mangroves, seagrass, oyster beds and marsh grasses. The two terms describe roles, not taxonomic groups.

Herbivore exclusion: an experiment, not just a correlation

The Hughes et al. (2007) example excludes grazing fishes using cages. Algae grows strongly where grazing is removed, compared with accessible reef surfaces. The mechanism is less consumption → more algal accumulation → more competition for coral space. This tests what grazers do, rather than simply noting that healthy reefs happen to have fish.

A cage can also alter flow, light or access by other animals. A strong design therefore needs comparable uncaged areas and procedural/partial-cage controls. Replicated treatments distinguish a grazing effect from a peculiar patch. The pictures illustrate the process; they do not supply a numerical effect size.

Succession, step by step

  1. A disturbance clears space; fast settlers and fast growers establish.
  2. Additional species arrive, initially increasing richness.
  3. Competition intensifies; dominant organisms form a canopy and exclude some competitors.
  4. Species capable of recruiting or growing under/alongside that canopy persist.

This is the sequence presented in the deck, not a guaranteed timetable for every reef. Repeated disturbance can interrupt it. Coral death also exposes substrate for algae; nutrients, rapidly spreading invasive algae and reduced grazing can accelerate the shift.

The complicated fishing diagram, translated

On slide 44, arrows marked − indicate suppression; + indicates enhancement. Dotted links include indirect effects. These are effects on another component, not arrows showing energy flowing toward a consumer.

  1. Humans remove predatory fish and herbivorous fish.
  2. Predatory fish suppress grazers, including urchins. Removing those predators can indirectly increase urchins.
  3. Fish and urchins both remove fleshy algae, which otherwise competes with crustose coralline algae (CCA).
  4. Both grazers can also erode carbonate/CCA. In this diagram, fish grazing's benefit from removing algae outweighs its erosion cost, whereas urchin erosion outweighs its algal-removal benefit.
  5. CCA supports reef structure, so changing the balance of fish, urchins and fleshy algae affects framework indirectly.

Why the simple chain fails: "fewer predators means more grazers means a better reef" ignores direct fishing of grazers and differences in erosion. The diagram's balance is context-specific, not a claim that urchins are always harmful. In Jamaica, losing Diadema reduced beneficial grazing.

Can a grazer help coral recruitment but reduce a carbonate budget?

Yes. Removing algae frees settlement space, but scraping also removes carbonate. Ecological benefits and geological costs can occur simultaneously; quantify both rather than calling all grazing good or bad.

Carbonate Budgets and Modelling

Written revision + official oral link

Calculate a budget with units and explain what it omits. Separate gross/net production, framework/sediment and stock/turnover. For the reef-island oral topic, connect production to transport, retention and risk rather than elevation alone.

A carbonate budget asks whether a reef is making more calcium carbonate than it loses. It connects organisms observed today with long-term framework, sediment, islands, and coastal protection.

The accounting

Net carbonate production = gross carbonate production − carbonate removal.

Common units are kg CaCO3 m−2 yr−1: kilograms of calcium carbonate per square metre per year. Positive means production exceeds removal; negative means removal exceeds production. A positive budget is not automatically the same number as vertical reef growth.

  • Producers: corals, CCA/other calcifying encrusters, molluscs, and sediment-producing calcifiers such as Halimeda.
  • Bioerosion: biological removal by scraping/excavating fish and urchins, macroboring sponges/worms, and microboring organisms.
  • Physical erosion: storms and breakage.
  • Chemical dissolution: carbonate dissolves under suitable chemical conditions. Ocean acidification is not an organism and is not itself "bioerosion".

Worked budget

Suppose corals produce 5.0 and encrusters produce 1.0 kg m−2 yr−1. Fish remove 2.0, urchins 0.5, and internal borers 1.0 in the same units.

  1. Gross production = 5.0 + 1.0 = 6.0.
  2. Measured bioerosion = 2.0 + 0.5 + 1.0 = 3.5.
  3. Net census budget = 6.0 − 3.5 = +2.5 kg m−2 yr−1.

If coral production drops to 1.0 and everything else remains unchanged, net becomes 2.0 − 3.5 = −1.5. This is a hypothetical calculation, not a field result. Unmeasured dissolution or physical export can make the full budget differ from the census estimate.

Framework versus sediment

Framework is the connected solid structure of the reef. Sediment is loose material, such as grains made when an organism scrapes skeleton. A parrotfish can remove material from the framework and produce sediment without removing that carbonate from the whole reef area.

Ask where the material goes next. It might fill spaces in the reef, move onto an island beach, or be carried out of your study area. For the framework budget, scraping is a loss. For a budget containing both framework and retained sediment, it can be an internal transfer. Your accounting boundary determines whether movement counts as loss.

Accretion is accumulation that builds the reef, often expressed as vertical growth per year. A mass rate is not yet a height rate: the same kilograms can form a dense compact layer or occupy more volume in a porous structure. Retention determines how much stays, and deposition determines where it builds up.

A simplified hypothetical conversion shows the units. Suppose 4 kg m−2 yr−1 is all retained in a uniform layer with effective bulk density 1,600 kg m−3. Thickness added = 4 ÷ 1,600 = 0.0025 m/yr = 2.5 mm/yr. At 50% retention, that example gives 1.25 mm/yr. A real conversion needs site-specific structure, losses and deposition; these assumed numbers are a teaching example, not a field result.

Three approaches to estimating production

  • Biological census budget: survey producers and eroders, then apply measured/published rates. Gives process detail but depends on assumptions.
  • Hydrochemical estimate: infer net calcification/dissolution from water chemistry and exchange. Interpretation depends on water movement and the area/time represented.
  • Reef cores: use dated skeleton/framework to reconstruct longer-term accumulation. A core integrates the past rather than only today's organisms.

ReefBudget: observations multiplied by rates

ReefBudget is a standardised census method introduced in 2012. It converts observations into annual carbonate production/removal by multiplying the amount of each producer or eroder by its estimated rate, then subtracting total removal from total production. It combines approximately 20 field measurements with published biological rates.

  • Corals: notes specify four to six 10 m transects recording substrate cover; combine cover with taxon-specific growth/calcification rates. Published rates simplify comparisons but may miss site-specific stress or growth differences.
  • Encrusters: measure cover and apply published production rates, or deploy substrates to measure local rates.
  • Urchins: abundance and size determine estimated erosion using feeding relationships.
  • Fish: abundance, size, species, bite rates, and erosion per bite determine removal. Stereo-video uses two cameras to estimate length; size matters because one large excavator is not equivalent to one small scraper.
  • Internal borers: substrate deployment with before/after CT scans, rubble analysis, or cover/rate estimates.
  • Sediment producers: identify components under a microscope and estimate abundance and turnover. This is difficult and uncertain.

Available fish methods include visual transects, diver-operated video, baited/unbaited cameras, point counts, and timed swims. Select the method for the quantity needed: abundance alone does not give bite activity or biomass.

Why islands are not passive objects

A reef island is often a body of sediment, not a fixed block with an unchanging outline. Organisms produce carbonate; erosion makes grains; waves and currents transport them. Depositing grains can build a shoreline, while export can erode it. The island may expand, contract or move even while sea level rises.

Two equally low islands can therefore have different risks. One might receive an ongoing sediment supply from a productive reef; another may have reduced production or disrupted transport. An island can retain land area but still suffer flooding or damage to infrastructure. Physical persistence is not the same as safe habitation.

The course's Pacific examples include islands that maintained or increased area, alongside more vulnerable settings. That is evidence against assuming uniform responses, not proof that all islands are safe. A model combines production, waves, sea level and transport assumptions to explore outcomes; its prediction remains conditional on those inputs.

A reef has high live-coral cover. Must its carbonate budget be positive?

No. Production depends on coral identity and growth, not cover alone, while fish, urchins, borers, physical loss, and dissolution also matter. High cover can coexist with high erosion.

The deck's budget interpretation bands

Slide 4 labels ≥2 kg CaCO3 m−2 yr−1 as healthy, 0.5-1.5 as borderline, and <0 as eroding. These are lecture rules of thumb, not universal diagnostic boundaries. The slide leaves some positive ranges unclassified. Do not invent categories for them. A negative result means estimated removal exceeds estimated production within the specified budget.

Turning sediment stocks into annual production

The deck lists foraminifera, bryozoans, molluscs, urchin skeletal material, crustaceans and coral-derived particles among sediment sources. Mineral identity matters: not all sponge spicules are calcium carbonate, so do not automatically count siliceous spicules as carbonate production.

Annual production = standing carbonate stock × annual turnover frequency. Stock is an amount per area; turnover frequency is replacements per year. If a producer has 0.8 kg CaCO3 m−2 and replaces that stock twice per year, estimated production is 1.6 kg CaCO3 m−2 yr−1. If turnover is supplied as a two-year interval instead, frequency is 1/2 per year and production is 0.4. Multiplying by the number of years would be wrong.

Fish survey details supplied by this deck

Your practical field book gives its own settling instruction; use the protocol named in the question rather than treating 15 minutes as universal.

The slide's log-4 abundance codes are bins: code 1 = 1 individual; 2 = 2-4; 3 = 5-16; 4 = 17-64; 5 = 65-256; 6 = 257-1,024; 7 = 1,025-4,096; 8 = 4,097-16,384. They are not raw counts. Code 4 does not mean four animals, and averaging codes is not the same as averaging abundances.

Why biological data alone cannot forecast an island

  1. Reconstruct history: cores/dating establish how the reef and island accumulated.
  2. Measure contemporary production: quantify carbonate sediment made around the island.
  3. Measure delivery: determine transport and supply to the island.
  4. Combine biology with environmental forcing: waves, sea level and high-energy events determine where material goes.

A reef can produce sediment without delivering it to a beach. Production, transport, retention and island stability are separate links.

Disturbance models and what they actually predict

A model is a set of rules connecting an initial condition to possible changes. In a reef model, an initial condition might include live-coral cover, coral sizes and substrate. Environmental inputs describe exposure, such as heat or storm disturbance. Rules represent processes such as mortality, growth, recruitment, breakage and erosion.

  1. Apply a disturbance. Some living tissue dies, or colonies break, according to the model's assumptions.
  2. Update the community. Live coral can become dead skeleton; broken material becomes rubble. Algae or recruits may occupy available surfaces.
  3. Recalculate functions. Less living calcifying tissue can lower production; retained dead skeleton may preserve some structure initially, before erosion removes it.
  4. Repeat and compare scenarios. Different exposure or management assumptions yield different trajectories.

This is the process logic, not a claim that all models use the same equations. Outputs can include dead coral, rubble, sand, algae, size distributions and rugosity. A model can therefore predict coral cover and structural complexity changing at different speeds.

The course scenarios include bleaching in Jakarta's Thousand Islands (2010) and cyclones at Orpheus Island (category 3 in 2006; category 5 in 2011). Planned additions such as COTS or acclimation require new process assumptions and evidence: naming a process does not mean it has already been implemented or validated.

Sediment pulse means a short event; chronic loading is repeated or sustained exposure. Equal sediment loading need not produce equal damage if communities have different histories and tolerances. The presented clear-water/turbid-reef comparison illustrates this: a naturally turbid community can respond differently, but that does not make further sediment harmless. Always distinguish the predicted response from the assumptions that generated it.

Local Threats and Management

Local pressures differ among reefs. An exam answer should connect pressure → mechanism → ecological effect → management response. "Pollution is bad" does not explain which process is damaged.

Coastal development and water quality

Construction can directly remove coral and change currents, sediment transport, runoff, sewage, and industrial discharge. Increased sediment can reduce light, smother surfaces, and interfere with feeding/settlement. Reef removal can increase coastal erosion and land retreat.

Singapore case: the notes report about 70% reef loss, high turbidity/sedimentation, coral depth contraction to less than 6 m, but limited loss of coral biodiversity. Five large colony-relocation projects had less than 11% survival in the example. Later approaches included substrate modification, artificial reefs, nurseries, and transplantation. These are case-specific outcomes, not a universal relocation success rate.

ICZM means integrated coastal zone management: coordinate environmental, cultural, and economic uses of the coastal zone rather than treating each project independently. Kaneohe Bay, Hawaii: sewage diversion/removal illustrates how addressing a major pressure can enable recovery; planting more coral without removing the pressure may fail.

Overharvesting and destructive fishing

Larger boats and stronger/more efficient gear can increase extraction. Removing fish changes food webs; destroying substrate removes habitat itself. Sea cucumbers are not fish, despite the PDF's "most over-harvested fish" phrase. Their exploitation removes sediment processing/nutrient recycling and income for coastal communities. Low adult density can also reduce fertilisation success in broadcast-spawning populations.

  • Blast fishing: explosives kill organisms and shatter reef framework.
  • Bottom trawling: gear dragged across the seabed can destroy habitat.
  • Cyanide fishing: chemical capture for aquarium/live-food trades can harm non-target organisms and coral. Notes date its Philippine aquarium-trade use to the early 1960s.
  • Ghost fishing: lost gear continues trapping organisms.

The lecture reports live fish worth up to 25 times dead fish and a Southeast Asian live-reef-fish market around US$1.2 billion in the cited context. Those incentives explain capture pressure; they are not current price guarantees.

Management options: enforced bans, affordable sustainable gear, livelihood alternatives, protection of fragile habitats, local stewardship, and credible fisheries certification. A ban without enforcement or alternatives may not change behaviour.

Oil, groundings, and plastics

Oil can damage tissue, impair feeding, reduce growth/reproduction, and accumulate through organisms. Shipping routes can cross important reefs. Douglas Shoal: the Shen Neng 1 grounded in April 2010; the notes record about five tonnes of oil spilled. Groundings also physically damage reef substrate, so spill volume does not capture the whole impact.

The PDF gives historical plastic estimates: eight million tonnes per year, about 80% land/rivers versus 20% marine sources, and concentration in a small number of rivers. Its "60-90% from 10 rivers" statistic has an unspecified denominator and should not be recited as a settled fraction of all ocean plastic. Plastic can entangle, abrade, smother, or be ingested. The annual oil-spill estimate is also inadequately contextualised in the notes.

Ballast water and invasive species

Ships take on water for stability, carrying organisms, eggs, cysts, and larvae. Discharge elsewhere can introduce non-native organisms. Establishment depends on survival and environmental match; being transported does not guarantee invasion. Management includes ballast-water treatment, filtration, UV/chemical technologies, and regulations.

Lionfish: native Indo-Pacific fish introduced to the western Atlantic/Caribbean, where predation on native prey and rapid reproduction are important concerns. Aquarium releases are the key pathway usually discussed; do not assume the PDF's generic ballast-water example establishes the actual lionfish pathway. Florida removal incentives are a management example.

The notes quote ten billion tonnes of ballast transported annually, "7,000 species each hour," and an invasion every nine weeks. The latter two lack adequate units/context; retain the concept, but check the lecture source before using them as quantitative evidence.

Antifouling

Biofouling is organisms growing on hulls. It increases drag/fuel use and can move species between regions. Antifoulants reduce attachment, but toxic compounds such as historical TBT (tributyltin) can damage non-target organisms. The management problem is to reduce fouling without transferring the cost to the ecosystem.

Crown-of-thorns and the GBR

Crown-of-thorns sea stars (COTS) are native coral predators, not automatically invasive species. At outbreak densities, stomach eversion and digestion remove large areas of coral tissue. The notes give 5-6 m2 consumption and survival for up to nine months without feeding, but the consumption figure has no time unit. Nutrient enrichment may improve larval food availability and survival, contributing to outbreaks; it is not the only control.

Historical statistic corrected: the 50.7% decline refers to GBR coral cover in 1985-2012, not the last 27 years from today. The study attributed 48% of the loss to cyclones, 42% to crown-of-thorns, and 10% to bleaching. The PDF's 24/21/5 approximates portions of the total starting cover, not those shares of loss. AIMS's account of the study provides the context.

People and sustainable use

GBR management includes Traditional Owners and enduring Sea Country relationships, climate action, land/water management, threatened species, heritage, and tourism. Recreational use can be extractive (fishing) or non-extractive (diving/snorkelling), but non-extractive does not mean impact-free. Anchoring, trampling, and wildlife disturbance can damage reefs.

Tools include carrying-capacity limits, permits, mooring buoys, appropriate infrastructure, user-pay systems, best-practice education, and enforcement. Tourism creates both pressure and incentives/income for conservation.

Why might coral transplantation fail near an untreated sewage outfall?

The original stressor remains: poor water quality can suppress survival, growth, or recruitment. Restoration adds organisms but does not remove the process damaging them. Pressure reduction should accompany intervention.

Land-based pollution is a watershed problem

A watershed is the land area draining toward a water body. Runoff can carry sediment, excess nutrients and agricultural chemicals to reefs. Sediment limits light or smothers surfaces; nutrient enrichment can stimulate algae; contaminants have their own toxic effects. The management response therefore includes erosion control, better fertiliser use, wastewater treatment and runoff reduction, not only rules imposed at the reef.

Assess effectiveness with outcomes: did pollutant delivery fall, water quality improve and organisms recover? Installing a treatment system is an action; reduced exposure and ecological recovery are outcomes. Lag times and other pressures can separate them.

Grounding damage is larger than spilled oil alone

Marginal and Extreme Reefs

Official oral link / marginal reefs

Marginal does not automatically mean degraded. Use an environmental/historical baseline, explain future conservation value and acknowledge limits. This distinction is explicitly part of one oral task.

Not every coral community resembles a clear-water tropical reef. Turbid, hot, cold/high-latitude, urban, mangrove/seagrass-associated, mesophotic, and CO2-seep communities can reveal how corals persist near environmental or ecological limits.

Three words that must not be collapsed

  • Extreme: in the lecture's proposed distinction, describes unusual/challenging environmental conditions such as temperature, acidity, or turbidity.
  • Marginal: describes ecological characteristics, for example low reef-building capacity or coral not being the dominant group. Other literature has used the word differently; state the course's distinction.
  • Degraded: implies a decline from an earlier condition. A naturally unusual community is not necessarily damaged.

Mesophotic describes deeper, low-light coral ecosystems. A CO2 seep releases CO2, providing a local natural analogue of some acidification conditions. Neither represents all future stresses simultaneously.

Why these sites matter

A natural analogue lets researchers study conditions resembling aspects of a future ocean. A resilience hotspot contains organisms/communities that persist or recover under stress. A refugium is a place providing relative protection/persistence. Survival in one extreme condition does not prove protection from every future condition.

Extreme sites can be natural laboratories and possible adaptive refugia. Marginal communities may have distinct biodiversity and conservation needs even without dominance by reef-building corals. Lower diversity does not make a community worthless.

Defining the categories is difficult

You need a reference baseline and measurements. A temperature extreme may occur daily, seasonally, or chronically; mean temperature alone misses variability. Historical ecological data are often absent. Labels without time scale, comparison, and evidence can confuse naturally persistent communities with recently damaged ones.

The notes say the 50 Reefs initiative included only one potential marginal reef in the lecture example, suggesting such communities can be overlooked. The full conservation-priority list is on unavailable slides, so it is not reconstructed here as if supplied.

Stradbroke Island case

Stradbroke supports a species-rich high-latitude coral community in turbid (particle-clouded) water. It has persisted episodically through about 7,000 years of the Holocene (the current geological epoch). Recent poorer water quality shifted composition from fast-growing Acropora toward smaller, slower-growing massive corals. Natural persistence and recent degradation can occur together; they are not mutually exclusive.

Is a coral community with naturally low coral cover necessarily degraded?

No. Degradation requires evidence of decline from an appropriate baseline. Low cover may be a persistent ecological characteristic. Compare historical data and environmental context rather than assuming the tropical clear-water reef is the only healthy reference.

Climate Change and Coral Reefs

Written revision / inferred

Work through DHW and acidification, and distinguish exposure from response. Explain acclimation versus adaptation using the Ofu comparison and transplant evidence. A laboratory heat score is not a lifetime guarantee.

Climate pressures act through different mechanisms: heat affects coral-symbiont function, CO2 changes carbonate chemistry, sea-level rise changes depth/coasts, and storms physically damage reefs. Distinguish them before connecting their combined effects.

Sea-level rise

Main mechanisms are thermal expansion of warming seawater and addition of water from melting land ice. Melting already-floating ice has little direct effect on sea level compared with land ice. Tide gauges measure local relative sea level; satellite altimetry gives broad ocean coverage. Land movement matters for local risk.

A king tide is a non-scientific term for an exceptionally high predictable tide. It is not itself evidence of climate change, but a higher mean sea level raises the level reached during high tides and storms. Reef growth and island sediment dynamics determine whether a system can keep pace.

Storms and regional sea-level variability

Storm waves break colonies and move sediment. El Niño changes winds, temperature, and regional sea level; some reefs can experience low water levels and exposure. Do not learn the PDF's wind-direction description as a universal explanation for every site. It also does not establish that all storms everywhere become more frequent.

Ocean acidification: what changes chemically?

Atmospheric CO2 dissolves in seawater, increasing dissolved CO2/carbonic-acid-related species and hydrogen ions. More H+ shifts carbonate toward bicarbonate, reducing carbonate-ion availability and aragonite saturation.

H+ + CO32− → HCO3−

H+ is a hydrogen ion; CO32− is carbonate; HCO3− is bicarbonate. Less available carbonate can make calcification harder and favour dissolution. Coral internal pH regulation does not erase this pressure. Acidification means pH decreases; the ocean does not have to fall below pH 7.

The notes quote a 26% acidity increase "since 1970" without a supporting source. The mechanism is examinable; verify the time baseline before using that percentage. Claims about fish sensory disruption should be treated as context/species dependent, not "all sensory systems crash".

Heat stress and bleaching

Elevated temperature, often interacting with strong light, disrupts symbiosis. Corals lose symbionts/pigment. Light-facing upper surfaces can bleach more than shaded surfaces. Bleaching is not automatically immediate mortality: duration, feeding, tolerance, and recovery matter.

Degree Heating Weeks, correctly unpacked

MMM means Maximum Monthly Mean: the climatological mean temperature of the warmest month at the location. HotSpot is temperature above MMM. NOAA accumulates HotSpot values of at least 1°C over the previous 12 weeks, expressing the sum in °C-weeks.

DHW = sum of qualifying daily HotSpots over 84 days ÷ 7.

Each daily term contributes its temperature excess only when the HotSpot is at least 1°C; other days contribute zero. The divisor converts daily accumulation to weeks. This is not simply the current week's temperature minus MMM, and degree-weeks are not seven times degree-days.

  1. MMM = 28°C; each of 14 days is 30°C.
  2. Each daily HotSpot = 30 − 28 = 2°C, which qualifies.
  3. Accumulation = 14 × 2 = 28°C-days.
  4. DHW = 28 ÷ 7 = 4°C-weeks, assuming other days contribute zero.

Historically, about 4°C-weeks indicates significant bleaching risk; around 8°C-weeks, widespread bleaching and mortality of heat-sensitive corals are likely. These are risk thresholds, not guarantees or literal temperature increases. NOAA's DHW explanation is the source for this correction.

Variability, acclimation, adaptation

The Ofu case emphasises temperature variability, not just averages. VBS means visual bleaching score, an observed condition measure rather than accumulated temperature exposure.

Acclimation: an individual's physiological adjustment to conditions, potentially within its lifetime. Adaptation: evolutionary change across generations through selection on heritable variation. A coral that survives one hot week has not thereby demonstrated genetic adaptation.

The course mnemonic MAAD is Move, Acclimate, Adapt, Die. Adult coral colonies are fixed, but populations can shift distribution through larval dispersal. Response options have limits when change is fast, habitat is unavailable, or reproduction is impaired.

The response portfolio

Reduce greenhouse-gas emissions, maintain traditional conservation/local pressure reduction, and consider interventions to improve resilience. Local management can help a reef withstand stress, but it cannot remove ocean-wide warming or acidification by itself.

Are 4 DHW the same as water warming by 4 degrees?

No. DHW integrates both intensity and duration of qualifying heat over a rolling window. Two degrees above MMM for two weeks gives four degree-weeks; it does not mean a four-degree instantaneous temperature anomaly.

The Ofu study: what was compared?

The deck contrasts Pool 300, a hotter, highly variable habitat, with Pool 400, a moderately variable habitat. Corals from the hotter habitat showed stronger heat resistance across the five species compared. Resistance was assessed with responses such as retained chlorophyll during stress, not merely the temperature where they were found.

A reciprocal transplant moves corals in both directions between the two pools and compares them with corals retained in their home habitats. This separates effects of destination conditions from differences between coral origins. In Palumbi et al. (2014), corals were followed over three years. Exposure to the warmer environment conferred about half the extra tolerance of the native warm-pool corals. This supports an environment-dependent component of tolerance. The remaining difference alone does not prove a particular genetic mechanism; origin, history and symbionts also need investigation.

Heat tolerance is a measurement, not a permanent badge

Heron reef-flat staghorns tested six months after the 2024 bleaching event were relatively resistant in the deck's comparison; the same species was less resistant in 2025. Species identity alone therefore does not guarantee an unchanged thermal response. Recent exposure, condition, host variation and symbiont associations can matter.

The coral host and its symbionts are separate biological components. Physiological acclimation, a changed symbiont association and genetic differences are different possible explanations. An experimental heat-ramp score is not a guarantee of survival through repeated field heatwaves, disease or storms.

Acidification affects more than adult corals

The deck uses Eastern oyster larvae (Talmage et al., 2010) to illustrate responses to different CO2 conditions. The link is carbonate chemistry and early shell formation: effects on development can occur even if an adult colony or shell is still present. Do not infer identical sensitivity across organisms or life stages.

Restoration and Future Options

Written revision + official oral link

Compare the four intervention categories and evaluate actual success. Use evidence plus limitations: diversity, heat-tolerance screening, survival versus ecosystem function, cost/scale and ongoing stress. This supports both restoration and assisted-evolution oral options.

Restoration is an intervention to help damaged reefs recover. It does not replace removing the pressures that caused damage. The lecture's three-part approach is: reduce local threats, reduce climate threats, and intervene where recovery needs help.

How restoration is done

  • Passive restoration: remove pressures, such as poor water quality or damaging fishing, so natural recovery can occur.
  • Direct transplantation: move corals directly onto a damaged reef.
  • Coral gardening: grow coral fragments in a nursery, then plant them on the reef.
  • Larval enhancement: collect/rear larvae and supply them to suitable reef surfaces to increase settlement.

What success should mean

Transplanted corals surviving the first year is only one outcome. Longer-term goals include growth, reproduction, recruitment, diversity, structural complexity, fish habitat, and carbonate production. A short-lived increase in coral cover does not demonstrate a self-sustaining reef.

The notes cite Laughing Bird Caye, Belize, as a large-scale success example, but also emphasise setbacks, variable first-year survival, high cost, and short projects, often 1-2 years. The student-note shorthand of approximately US$360,000/ha should not be learned as one universal average: the actual review chart below separates technique-specific medians, with gardening around $352,000/ha and direct transplantation around $218,000/ha in 2010 dollars. Longer monitoring helps reveal persistence, but duration alone does not guarantee success.

Principle 1: diversify the portfolio

Include different species, growth forms, and genotypes/colonies. If all transplants respond identically to heat or disease, one event can remove the whole investment. Monoculture contains one cultivated type; polyculture mixes types.

  • Mounding/massive corals: often slower-growing and relatively stress tolerant.
  • Branching corals: often fast-growing and structurally complex but vulnerable to some disturbances.
  • Plates/fingers: described as weedy/opportunistic in the lecture; traits vary among species.

The lecture reports an area with 15 coral species having five times the fish-species richness and twice the number of fish compared with the relevant comparison area, and 33% greater coral growth in polyculture than monoculture. The comparator/site details are absent, so do not convert these into universal causal multipliers. The principle is that diversity and structure can support function and spread risk.

Principle 2: build for future conditions

If the original coral community was lost because it could not withstand heat, planting only similarly vulnerable corals can recreate the problem. Regeneration here means rebuilding with future conditions in mind: retain ecosystem function while considering tolerance, diversity and likely future exposure, rather than copying a past species list regardless of risk.

Screening tests candidate colonies before using them. For example, expose comparable fragments to the same heat treatment and compare bleaching responses. A high-performing candidate is evidence of relative tolerance under that test, not immunity to every future stress. Its growth, reproduction and ecological role still matter.

Chlorophyll per surface area is a normalised pigment measurement: divide measured chlorophyll by the tissue/colony surface area represented. A large fragment might contain more total pigment just because it is larger; normalisation makes comparisons more meaningful. Such measurements can correlate with thermal response, but a correlation neither establishes the causal mechanism nor supplies a universal cut-off for selecting corals.

A quartile is one quarter of a ranked group. The highest heat-resistance quartile is the best-performing 25% in that comparison, not a claim of 25% extra tolerance. Retest after transplantation to ask whether rankings persist; the common-garden evidence below gives the actual proportions rather than assuming they all remain at the top.

Movement creates trade-offs. A coral from a hot site may tolerate heat yet perform poorly under a destination's light, sediment or disease conditions. Moving organisms can also move pathogens and genes, so environmental matching, biosecurity, local adaptation and permission matter. The course discussion's 10-100 km movement range is an opinion range, not a universal safe distance or permit.

Evaluate an intervention, not just its photograph

  1. What stressor is being addressed, and has it been reduced?
  2. Which species/genotypes and growth forms are used, and why?
  3. What is the comparison/control and pre-intervention baseline?
  4. How long is monitoring, and what counts as functional recovery?
  5. What are costs, scalability, risks, and responsibilities?
Why not plant only the fastest-growing branching coral?

Fast initial cover is useful but can trade off with stress tolerance and leave the reef vulnerable to a shared disturbance. Mixed growth forms/species/genotypes spread risk and can support more habitat functions. Choose for long-term conditions, not only first-year growth.

The four intervention categories you should be able to explain

1. Genetic and reproductive
Managed selection chooses existing resistant colonies. Supportive breeding breeds corals under managed conditions to supply offspring; outcrossing combines parents from different populations; hybridisation combines different species. Gamete/larval capture and seeding increases recruitment opportunities. Cryopreservation freezes reproductive material for later use. Genetic manipulation changes genes in coral or symbionts. Selection, breeding and gene editing are not synonyms.
2. Physiological
Pre-exposure conditions corals with stress before a later challenge, aiming to increase tolerance. Symbiont or microbiome manipulation changes associated algae or microbes. Antibiotics target bacteria; phage therapy uses viruses that infect bacteria; antioxidants limit oxidative damage; nutritional supplements provide extra food/nutrients. These target function or condition rather than necessarily changing the host's inherited DNA.
3. Population and community
Managed relocation moves organisms. Assisted gene flow moves genetic variation among populations; assisted migration moves organisms toward suitable future habitat, potentially into new areas. Risks include disease transfer, environmental mismatch and ecological effects.
4. Environmental
Atmospheric/marine shading reduces light exposure; cool-water mixing targets heat; abiotic acidification interventions target chemistry; seagrass and macroalgal beds can alter local conditions. Local feasibility and side effects matter, and these do not eliminate global emissions.

What the biotechnology examples establish

CRISPR/Cas9 is a gene-editing tool. Cleves et al. (2018), shown in the deck, tested gene manipulation including fluorescent-protein/growth-related targets. Mosaic uptake means not all cells carry the same edit. Target choice, editing success, long generation times and unknown risks limit translation from gene-function experiments to reef-scale restoration. The slide's "one paper" is its historical framing, not a count of all research today.

The symbiont experiment describes strains evolved at 31°C with better growth/photosynthesis and lower toxic ROS than wild type under the tested conditions. ROS means reactive oxygen species: chemically reactive molecules that can damage cells when regulation fails. The Rosado et al. (2018) trial supplied selected microbes to corals and tested bleaching responses: the intervention changes the microbial community, rather than editing coral DNA. Neither lab example establishes a universally effective treatment for all field reefs.

Larval enhancement has 22.2% median survival in the chart; this comparison is not a randomised trial proving that technique alone caused the difference. Site, methods, conditions and how survival was measured can differ. Many duration medians are approximately 1-2 years, leaving long-term persistence under-observed.

Common-garden evidence: does a resistant colony stay resistant?

A common garden places colonies of different origins/genotypes in a shared environment, reducing environmental differences in the comparison. In the deck's Armstrong work (labelled in preparation), 40% of initial top-quartile colonies remained in the top quartile, and 70% remained in the top half. Top quartile means best 25%; top half means best 50%. It does not mean 70% remained in the best 25%.

Those outcomes suggest partial persistence of ranking, not certainty for every colony. Diversity remains valuable because heat tolerance, growth, reproduction, disease response and habitat function are not the same trait.

Super Reefs: predict, prove, protect, repeat

  1. Predict: use models to identify candidate resistant locations or colonies.
  2. Prove: test local resistance rather than trusting a model alone.
  3. Protect: support those resources through measures such as nurseries and MPAs.
  4. Repeat: expand testing and protection, checking outcomes as conditions change.

The Palau example involves heat-resistant corals for reefs damaged by a typhoon; the July 2025 nursery/supply-chain example is multispecies. They illustrate implementation and partnerships, not proof that warming is solved. The deck ends with the scaling chain: identify a problem, test a solution, develop it, make it understandable/affordable/scalable, then spread it.

The urchin protocol

A second observer follows along the same belt, carefully searching crevices in a criss-cross path. Record species and test/body size excluding spines: 0-20, 21-40, 41-60, 61-80, 81-100 mm, etc. A long spine does not mean a large test. Search effort matters because hidden individuals can be missed.

Field day 3

Benthic Cover and Structural Complexity

Benthos here includes living and non-living reef-surface elements. Cover measures which surface categories occur; rugosity measures the surface's three-dimensional complexity. A reef can have similar cover but different structure.

Course LIT protocol

  1. Work in pairs on one 20 m transect. Follow a consistent depth contour, roughly parallel to the island/reef front; handbook transects are at least 5 m apart.
  2. Secure the tape so it is not moving in the current. It generally spans the surface rather than tracing every small bump; follow the slope when depth changes by more than 1 m.
  3. Look directly below the line and record category lengths to the nearest centimetre.
  4. For live coral, also measure the contoured surface-profile length with flexible tape. The handbook calls this "2D surface area", but its sheets record a length in cm, not an area in cm2.
  5. Photograph every 1 m, from directly above, preferably 0.5 m and at most about 1 m above the tape. Keep centimetre marks and benthos visible.
  6. Record date/time, depth, weather, and start/end GPS coordinates; check that category lengths sum to 20 m / 2,000 cm.

Codes link observations to carbonate calculations

Major codes: SD sand, RU rubble, RC rock, DC dead coral, SP sponge, OT other, MA algae, HC live hard coral, and SC soft coral.

Algal subcodes: MAC macroalgae, RCTF turf, RCCA crustose coralline algae. Hard-coral subcodes: HCB branching, HCC corymbose, HCE encrusting, HCM massive, HCF foliose/frondose, HCP plate.

These are course sheet codes, not a universal taxonomic standard. Correct categories matter because models use different growth rates, densities, and relationships for different taxa/forms. Turf on dead framework may require a specific category decision; follow the taught coding protocol rather than double-counting the same length.

Rugosity: what the chain is doing

Lay a chain over the contours, including rises and depressions. Measure the straight distance between its endpoints. The contoured route is longer than its straight span.

Rugosity = contoured chain length ÷ straight endpoint distance.

Example: a 500 cm chain spans 250 cm horizontally. Rugosity = 500 ÷ 250 = 2.0. On a flat surface the distances are equal, giving 1. Greater values mean greater surface complexity under this convention.

Handbook inconsistency: printed p. 33 reverses/mislabels the lengths in its written formula while saying values above 2 indicate high rugosity. That conclusion fits the contoured/straight ratio above. Confirm the requested convention with the instructor; reversing the ratio gives values at or below 1. The course measures complexity near the start and end of each transect with a 5 m chain.

Data quality and interpretation

Use consistent filenames containing date, site, transect, and metre position. Ensure the line, tape marks, and substrate are legible. Record visibility and logger information as relevant. Separating live cover from complexity helps detect the difference between tissue loss and later framework erosion.

Compare LIT with photo quadrats.

LIT directly records category lengths under a line, including the taught coral profile measurement, but relies on field classification and can be slow. Photo quadrats archive a sampled area for repeated annotation, but require clear imagery and later analysis. Their sampling geometry differs, so similar cover estimates do not mean identical observations.

Field day 4

Coral Growth with Photogrammetry

Structure from motion (SfM) reconstructs 3D geometry from overlapping photographs taken at different positions. It estimates camera poses and shared points, then builds a model. Repeating a scaled, comparable model can reveal changes in coral shape or volume without collecting the colony.

Colony selection

The course selects six Acropora colonies per group; each pair works on one. Select healthy, unbleached colonies without substantial tissue loss, ideally 20-40 cm diameter, with camera access on all sides and hard dead substrate for markers. Very small colonies may grow differently; very large/inaccessible colonies are harder to image. Check selection with staff.

Place three permanent markers, ideally in a triangle near the colony, in dead substrate rather than living organisms. Markers allow re-identification and comparable framing. Put a metal ruler nearby: without a known length, a photogrammetric model has no reliable real-world scale.

Image collection

  1. Photograph the colony identifier and a top-down reference showing the coral, all three markers, and ruler.
  2. Take overlapping images from all angles, maintaining useful distance and clear focus.
  3. Use two or three circles around the colony, at least 15 photos per circle, plus views over the top to the opposite base.
  4. The general introduction mentions 50-150 photos, but the detailed practical asks for at least 100 good images per colony.
  5. Check that the ruler/markers are well represented and that the sequence covers the colony without missing sides.

Metashape: what each stage achieves

  1. Quality check: disable irrelevant/blurred photos. Handbook image-quality scores above 0.5 are ideal, 0.3-0.5 need inspection, below 0.3 are disabled.
  2. Align photos: find matching features and estimate camera positions; produce sparse tie points. Aim for at least 100 aligned images in this practical.
  3. Optimise cameras: refine the estimated camera geometry.
  4. Clean tie points: remove high-error matches without making holes by deleting too much; re-optimise. The handbook targets error below 0.7 pixels while retaining many projections.
  5. Build the point cloud: reconstruct more detailed 3D points; the taught settings are Medium quality and Aggressive depth filtering.
  6. Scale: use three known ruler intervals as scale bars. Convert centimetres to metres in the software: 5 cm is 0.05 m, not 5 m.
  7. Check scaling error: workbook target below 0.001 m / 1 mm; record it in metadata.
  8. Georeference: enter known coordinates, update the transform, save workspace, and export the report/point cloud.
The workbook's detailed tie-point settings

How growth is inferred

Volume change = later volume − earlier volume. Divide by elapsed time for a volume-change rate. Example: 1,200 to 1,320 cm3 over one year gives +120 cm3 yr−1, or 10% relative to the starting volume. This is not automatically a calcium-carbonate mass rate: density, tissue/framework interpretation, and model accuracy matter.

Negative change may indicate breakage, partial mortality/erosion, or inconsistent models, scale, coverage, or segmentation. Do not interpret a tiny difference below measurement uncertainty as definite biological shrinkage.

Field day 6

Heat-Stress Experiments: VBS and ED50

This experiment compares coral fragments under a standardised temperature ramp. It tests relative heat responses among colonies/species under controlled conditions. It does not prove how every colony will perform through years of real-world warming.

Design: which samples belong together?

Each pair samples six colonies of a selected common coral species, taking four fragments per colony, each about 2-3 cm. One fragment from each colony goes into each of four treatment tanks: 30°C control, 34°C, 36°C, 38°C. That gives 24 fragments per pair. Keep colony identities: fragments from the same colony share a biological source.

The ramp starts at 30°C for one hour, rises over three hours, holds its maximum for three hours, and returns to 30°C. The handbook also says approximately 11 am-6 pm; that abbreviated clock description does not specify a separate cooling duration. Score the response the following afternoon, rather than mixing different recovery periods.

Visual Bleaching Score (VBS)

  1. 1: no bleaching.
  2. 2: barely visible bleaching.
  3. 3: obvious bleaching.
  4. 4: severe but incomplete bleaching, mostly white with some colour.
  5. 5: complete bleaching.

Compare with controls and photograph fragments against the colour reference in a temperature-by-colony layout. Record student/sample IDs, species, collection site/date, analysis date, and treatment. VBS is an ordinal visual score; moving from 1 to 2 need not equal the biological change from 4 to 5.

What ED50 represents in this practical

ED50 means the estimated treatment temperature associated with the chosen midpoint bleaching response. The workbook uses VBS = 3 as an operational proxy for "50% bleached". That is not the same as a directly measured 50% loss of symbiont cells, nor a claim that half the colonies died.

Fit VBS against temperature:

VBS = intercept + slope × temperature.

Then solve for the temperature at score 3:

ED50 = (3 − intercept) ÷ slope.

Intercept is the fitted score at temperature zero in the mathematical line, not a real coral observation at 0°C. Slope is score change per degree. Example: VBS = −14.5 + 0.5 × T gives ED50 = (3 + 14.5) ÷ 0.5 = 35°C.

A lower ED50 indicates greater sensitivity under this test; a higher ED50 suggests the midpoint response requires more heat. Calculate colony estimates, mean, variance, and range. If the slope is zero or the fitted midpoint falls outside tested temperatures, the estimate may be undefined or an uncertain extrapolation. The simplified linear fit and ordinal response should not be overinterpreted.

How to improve a pilot experiment

Increase independent colonies and replicate tanks, randomise positions, verify actual temperatures, standardise fragment size/light/handling/recovery, use blinded or repeated scoring, and add physiological measurements and longer follow-up. One tank per temperature confounds treatment with tank: many fragments in that tank do not provide independent tank replication.

Why is ED50 not DHW?

ED50 is an experimentally estimated temperature at a response threshold, in degrees Celsius. DHW integrates qualifying field heat anomalies over time, in degree-weeks. One measures a test response; the other measures accumulated environmental exposure.

Written and oral prompts

1. Explain how a reef can be productive in nutrient-poor tropical water.

Define the coral-symbiont mutualism; explain photosynthetic carbon transfer and recycling of nutrients; add heterotrophic/food-web pathways. Low water-column nutrients do not imply no nutrient cycling within a reef. Avoid claiming that corals photosynthesise themselves.

2. Compare broadcast spawning and brooding, then explain a recruitment bottleneck.

Spawners release gametes and fertilisation occurs in the water. Brooders retain early development and release larvae. Larvae disperse, settle, and become juvenile polyps. Suitable substrate, algal competition, predation, and mortality affect successful recruitment.

3. A satellite map shows less coral than a diver survey. Give three possible explanations and a test.

Different spatial footprints/resolution, depth or turbidity obscuring the bottom, classification errors, different dates, or biased diver placement. Align place/time/method and validate against independent observations across habitats; do not assume either method is automatically correct.

4. Corals produce 4.8 and CCA 0.7. Fish erode 2.2, urchins 0.8, borers 1.1. Calculate and interpret net production.

Gross = 5.5; erosion = 4.1; net = +1.4 kg CaCO3 per square metre per year if inputs share those units. Positive production does not directly provide vertical accretion or account for every export/dissolution process.

5. Explain why clear-water and naturally turbid reefs may respond differently to a sediment pulse.

Different historical exposure, species composition, and tolerances matter. Sediment reduces light and can smother/impair feeding or settlement. A naturally turbid reef may resist some added sediment better, but it is not immune; duration and intensity still matter.

6. Contrast resistance and resilience using bleaching.

Resistance: smaller bleaching/damage under the same heat stress. Resilience: recovery after damage, such as restored symbiosis, growth, or recruitment. Compare the same stress and a defined recovery time; surviving once is not proof of genetic adaptation.

7. A site has 28 qualifying days 1.5 degrees above MMM. Calculate DHW. What happens to the calculation on days only 0.5 degrees above MMM?

28 times 1.5 / 7 = 6 degree-weeks, assuming other contributions are zero. The 0.5-degree HotSpot days do not qualify for NOAA DHW accumulation because the threshold is 1 degree. Do not multiply daily accumulation by 7.

8. Design a response to algae dominance after coral loss.

Diagnose the pressure: nutrients, loss of grazers, poor water quality, or repeated disturbance. Reduce the causes; protect herbivory and suitable settlement substrate; monitor coral and algae with replicated standardised methods. Restoration may assist but cannot substitute for pressure reduction.

9. Why should an extreme coral community not automatically be restored into a clear-water reef community?

It may be naturally persistent and have distinct ecological/conservation value. Establish environmental and historical baselines first. A clear-water assemblage may be poorly suited to local conditions. Distinguish marginal/extreme from degraded.

10. Assess a one-year restoration trial with high survival of one coral species.

High survival is encouraging but limited. Need controls, longer monitoring, reproduction/recruitment, diversity, heat tolerance, structure, carbonate budget, costs, and whether the original stressor is reduced. A monoculture may carry shared vulnerability.

11. True or false: a non-extractive reef activity has no ecological impact.

False. Diving, snorkelling, boating, and anchoring can cause disturbance or physical damage. Non-extractive means not deliberately removing organisms/resources; it does not mean no effect.

12. True or false: all carbonate eroded from framework is lost from the reef system.

False. Framework can become sediment retained locally, filling gaps or nourishing islands. Export and dissolution remove it from the defined system; the boundary and budget compartment matter.

13. Explain the fishing diagram without saying all herbivores are good.

Trace direct removal of predators and grazers, indirect release of urchins, algal removal, and carbonate erosion. The CCA response depends on the balance. Compare with Jamaica to show context, not a universal anti-urchin rule.

14. Design a test of whether grazing controls algal growth.

Use replicated comparable grazed and exclusion treatments, baseline measurements and cage-artifact controls. Measure algal change consistently. Explain that a cage can alter flow/light as well as grazing.

15. Why combine environmental and biological information in a carbonate/island model?

Producer and eroder data estimate material generation/removal. Waves, currents, storms and sea level govern transport and retention. Cores give history. An island depends on sediment arriving and remaining, not simply being produced.

16. Explain all four restoration categories with one mechanism and one limitation each.

Genetic/reproductive: selection or breeding changes the future stock, but generation times/risks matter. Physiological: conditioning or microbial/symbiont interventions target tolerance, but field persistence varies. Population/community: relocation supplies organisms or genes, but mismatch/disease are risks. Environmental: shading/mixing changes exposure, but scale and side effects limit use.

17. A heat-resistant colony stays high-ranked in a common garden. What can you conclude?

The result supports some persistence under shared conditions; it does not guarantee all future stress responses or identify one genetic mechanism by itself. State sample size, uncertainty, conditioning history and possible symbiont effects.

18. Why is a one-year survival percentage inadequate evidence of reef restoration?

Survival does not establish reproduction, recruitment, biodiversity, carbonate production or persistence through later heatwaves. State monitoring duration, comparison/control, costs, functions and continued stressor reduction.

An answer that explains, rather than names

Question: Why does losing herbivores threaten coral recovery?

Answer: Herbivores remove algae from reef surfaces. When grazing falls, algae can expand, occupy settlement space, and shade or otherwise compete with corals. That can reduce recruitment after coral mortality and reinforce an algal state. The Jamaican Diadema decline is the course example. Protecting herbivores and reducing nutrient pollution address different controls on the same competition.

1. How a coral works

Animal, algae, skeleton: three different things

A polyp is the animal: tentacles capture food, the mouth leads to the digestive cavity, and living tissue covers its skeleton. A corallite is one polyp's skeletal cup; a corallum is the whole colony skeleton. Many connected polyps form a colony. A coral colony is not one species' entire population.

Zooxanthellae are symbiotic dinoflagellates, commonly classified in Symbiodiniaceae. They live in the coral's tissue. Photosynthesis turns inorganic carbon into organic compounds using light. Some products pass to the animal and supply energy for maintenance, growth, reproduction and calcification. The animal supplies habitat and recycled compounds, including CO2 and nitrogen nutrients. Both benefit: mutualism.

Heterotrophy is consuming organic food. Tentacles and nematocysts capture prey; a cnidocyte is the stinging cell and a nematocyst is its dischargeable capsule. Feeding can partly compensate for lost photosynthetic input, but not equally in all coral species. Reefs can be productive in nutrient-poor water because recycling and symbiosis retain and reuse nutrients.

Calcification and the vocabulary

Ca2+ + CO32− → CaCO3

Calcium ions plus carbonate ions form calcium carbonate; reef-building coral skeleton is aragonite. This is simplified chemistry. Corals regulate their calcifying environment, including pH, but are not immune to external acidification.

Calice: upper cup region. Theca: its enclosing wall. Septa: inward skeletal plates. Costae: external ribs. Colony growth form (branching, massive, plate, encrusting) differs from corallite arrangement: plocoid cups have separate walls; cerioid cups share walls; meandroid mouths form shared-wall valleys; phaceloid cups form separate tubes. The full skeletal list includes the remaining arrangements.

Reproduction and species

Sexual: eggs and sperm fertilise to form larvae. Broadcast spawners release gametes into water; brooders retain early development before releasing larvae. A planula disperses, settles and becomes a juvenile polyp. Settlement and early survival are bottlenecks. Asexual: budding adds polyps; fragmentation creates colonies from surviving pieces. Fragments are clones of their source, not new sexual crosses.

Shape alone does not establish species identity: Pavona maldivensis can be plate-like or columnar. Indo-Pacific and Caribbean assemblages contain different species pools; the Coral Triangle is especially species-rich. Caribbean A. prolifera is a hybrid of A. palmata and A. cervicornis. F1 means direct offspring of that cross; subsequent fragmentation does not create F2. Hybrids are not automatically sterile.

Bleaching is not death

Heat, often with strong light, disrupts the coral-algal partnership. Losing algae and/or their pigments exposes the pale skeleton through living tissue: bleaching. The animal may recover, but prolonged energy shortage can kill it. Resistance is less damage during stress; resilience is recovery afterwards. Heat-tolerant symbionts can help, sometimes with growth trade-offs.

Explain the consequence: bleaching → possible mortality → lower living cover and calcification → later erosion of dead skeleton → reduced shelter and altered fish communities. Structure can remain immediately after tissue dies, so cover and habitat complexity need not fall together.

2. Grazing, competition and ecological change

Algae and corals compete for limited space and light. Grazing removes algae, helping leave settlement space for coral larvae. Excess nutrients can increase algal production (bottom-up control); losing consumers reduces removal (top-down control). Both can favour algae.

Jamaica: the 1980s loss of Diadema urchins reduced grazing; algae expanded and coral recruitment was inhibited. This is a causal pathway, not merely an association between algae and unhealthy reefs. A phase shift is a change in the dominant community, such as coral to macroalgae. A positive feedback amplifies change: algae obstructing coral recovery can help maintain the algal state. Positive does not mean good.

Herbivore-exclusion experiment, Hughes et al. (2007): cages exclude grazing fish; algae accumulates more than on accessible surfaces. Manipulating grazing tests its effect. However, cages also change flow/light/access, so partial-cage controls, comparable uncaged patches and replication help separate these effects.

A trophic cascade is an indirect effect across feeding levels. Removing predators can release urchins, but fishing can simultaneously remove grazing fish. Fish and urchins also scrape carbonate/CCA. The lecture diagram assigns fish a net benefit to CCA and urchins a net erosion cost in that setting. Do not generalise that balance to all reefs: Jamaica demonstrates beneficial urchin grazing.

CCA (crustose coralline algae) can bind framework and provide settlement cues; Halimeda makes carbonate sediment; excessive fleshy macroalgae can obstruct recruitment. "All algae are bad" is therefore wrong. Likewise, a parrotfish can improve settlement conditions while removing carbonate: ecological benefit and geological cost are different outcomes.

Other essential distinctions: a foundation species structures a community, often by supplying habitat; an ecosystem engineer changes the physical environment. Corals can be both. Succession is community change after disturbance. The intermediate disturbance hypothesis proposes greatest diversity at intermediate disturbance: little disturbance can permit competitive exclusion, while excessive disturbance prevents establishment. It is a context-dependent hypothesis, not a rule to damage reefs.

3. Carbonate budgets and reef islands

Net carbonate production = gross production − removal. Units are usually kg CaCO3 m−2 yr−1: kilograms per square metre per year. Positive means estimated production exceeds removal; negative means the reverse.

Corals, CCA, molluscs and calcifying sediment producers add carbonate. Fish/urchin scraping and internal borers remove framework (bioerosion). Storm breakage is physical erosion; dissolution is chemical loss. Acidification itself is not an eroding organism.

Worked budget: coral production 5 + encrusters 1 = gross 6. Fish erosion 2 + urchins 0.5 + borers 1 = removal 3.5. Net = 6 − 3.5 = +2.5 kg CaCO3 m−2 yr−1. If coral production falls to 1, net becomes 2 − 3.5 = −1.5. Do not add removal or mix units.

Stock versus production: stock is carbonate already present; production is an amount per time. Annual production = stock × annual turnover frequency. A stock of 0.8 kg/m2 replaced every two years has frequency 0.5/year and production 0.4 kg/m2/year, not 1.6.

ReefBudget surveys organisms/cover/size and applies biological production or erosion rates. Fish counts alone cannot give erosion: size, identity, feeding activity and removal per bite matter. A hydrochemical method uses water chemistry/exchange to infer net calcification; dated cores reconstruct longer-term accumulation.

Framework is connected solid reef; sediment is loose grains. Scraping can transfer carbonate from framework to sediment without exporting it from the reef. State your accounting boundary. Positive mass production is not automatically vertical accretion: retention, porosity and deposition determine how much height is added.

Island chain: carbonate production → grain formation → wave/current transport → delivery → deposition and retention. Producing grains does not guarantee that they reach or remain on an island. Equally low islands can have different risks because supply, wave exposure, geometry and infrastructure differ. An island can retain/gain area but lose habitability through flooding or freshwater problems. Assess budgets, repeat shorelines/elevations, sediment movement and wave/sea-level records together.

The lecture's budget bands are ≥2 healthy, 0.5-1.5 borderline, <0 eroding in these units. These are course rules of thumb; the slide does not classify every positive value.

4. Heat, acidification and tolerance

DHW: intensity multiplied by duration

MMM is the climatological mean temperature of the warmest month at that site. HotSpot is temperature minus MMM. Accumulate daily HotSpots only when they are at least 1°C over the previous 84 days, then divide by 7. DHW is Degree Heating Weeks, measured in °C-weeks, not current temperature.

Example: MMM 28°C, temperature 30°C for 14 days, otherwise no qualifying exposure. HotSpot = 2°C. DHW = (14 × 2) ÷ 7 = 4°C-weeks. A day 0.5°C above MMM contributes zero. A qualifying day 2°C above MMM contributes 2, not 1.

Historically, around 4 DHW indicates significant bleaching risk and 8 DHW widespread bleaching with mortality risk for sensitive corals. They are risk thresholds, not guarantees, nor four/eight degrees of instantaneous warming.

Acidification and sea level

Dissolving atmospheric CO2 increases hydrogen ions. H+ + CO32− → HCO3−: hydrogen combines with carbonate to make bicarbonate. Less carbonate and lower aragonite saturation make skeleton/shell formation harder and can favour dissolution. Acidification means decreasing pH, not necessarily pH below 7. Larvae forming early shells can be affected as well as adults.

Sea-level rise comes mainly from warming-water expansion and melting land ice. Local relative sea level also depends on land movement. Storms break colonies and redistribute sediment. A king tide is a predictable high tide, not proof of climate change; a higher mean sea level raises the level reached during tides/storms.

What Ofu actually shows

Acclimation: physiological adjustment within an individual's lifetime. Adaptation: evolutionary change across generations involving heritable variation. In Ofu, hotter/more variable Pool 300 corals were more heat resistant than those in moderately variable Pool 400.

The reciprocal transplant moved corals both ways and compared them with home-habitat corals. Following them over three years, exposure to the warmer habitat conferred about half the extra tolerance of native warm-pool corals. This supports an environmental component; the remaining difference alone does not establish a particular genetic explanation. Symbionts and exposure history also matter.

MAAD: Move, Acclimate, Adapt, Die. Adult colonies cannot swim away, but larval dispersal can shift populations. A high heat-test score is not guaranteed resistance to disease, acidification or repeated future heatwaves.

5. Restoration: mechanisms, evidence and limits

Passive restoration removes pressures so recovery can occur; active restoration directly assists recovery. Direct transplantation moves colonies onto damaged reef; coral gardening grows fragments in nurseries before outplanting; larval enhancement supplies larvae to increase settlement. None removes global warming.

  • Genetic/reproductive: select naturally tolerant corals, breed them, supply larvae, freeze reproductive material, or manipulate genes. Selection chooses existing variation; gene editing changes DNA.
  • Physiological: precondition with stress, alter symbionts/microbes, or improve nutrition/condition. These need not change inherited coral DNA.
  • Population/community: move organisms. Assisted gene flow introduces genetic variation among populations; assisted migration moves organisms toward suitable future habitat. Risks include disease transfer and environmental mismatch.
  • Environmental: shading, cooling/mixing or local chemistry interventions change exposure. Feasibility, side effects and scale constrain them.

Diversify: mix species, growth forms and genotypes (distinct genetic makeups). Fast-growing branching corals rebuild structure quickly but may share vulnerabilities; massive corals often grow more slowly and tolerate some stresses better. Many fragments of one clone are many colonies, not high genetic diversity.

Build for the future: screen tolerance, match corals to destination conditions and retest. In the lecture's common-garden work, 40% of initial top-quartile corals remained top quartile, while 70% remained in the top half. Top quartile means best 25%. These in-preparation results suggest partial persistence, not immunity or proof of one genetic mechanism.

Costs: the review chart gives medians in 2010 US dollars per hectare: direct transplantation $218,305; gardening $351,661; larval enhancement $523,162. Different techniques/sites/sample sizes prevent a universal ranking of value. Direct transplantation's illustrated median survival is 64%, with median project duration one year; short monitoring misses later failure.

Scale calculation: 255,000 km2 × 100 = 25.5 million hectares. Restoring 1% means 255,000 ha. At the rounded historical $218,000/ha benchmark, 255,000 × 218,000 = $55.59 billion. This is illustrative, not a current global budget.

Success: measure survival, growth, reproduction, recruitment, diversity, habitat complexity and carbonate production over multiple years, against baselines/comparison areas. Recruitment means new juveniles establishing, not just repeatedly planting replacements. A self-sustaining reef is a stronger outcome than a surviving collection of transplants. Detailed evidence and your argument follow in the oral section.

6. Identification and measuring the right thing

Fish and invertebrate distinctions

Fish form relates to function: fusiform streamlined for sustained swimming; laterally compressed thin side-to-side for reef manoeuvring; depressed flattened top-to-bottom; anguilliform eel-like. Colour alone is a poor identification key.

Damselfish: one nostril per side, interrupted lateral line. Butterflyfish: compressed body and small mouth. Angelfish: strong preopercular spine near the gill cover. Parrotfish: fused beak-like teeth, grazing/bioerosion. Wrasses: canine teeth and often pectoral-fin swimming; some are cleaners. Triggerfish: locking first dorsal spine. Porcupinefish: inflation and raised spines. Review the remaining fish examples and parrotfish plate; the supplied sources do not contain the promised full 26-family/28-species key.

Sponges: pores admit water, choanocytes capture particles, oscula release water. Molluscs: muscular foot, organ-containing visceral mass, mantle and often shell; radula is a feeding ribbon, absent in bivalves. Echinoderms: usually five-part adult symmetry and water-powered tube feet; an urchin's test is its body skeleton and Aristotle's lantern its jaw. Arthropods: jointed limbs and exoskeleton, with moulting for growth. Fan worms are annelids; tunicates are invertebrate chordates, not arthropods or vertebrates.

Variable → method → calculation

  • Cover: LIT measures category lengths under a line; point-intercept classifies fixed points. 3.2 m live coral / 10 m × 100 = 32%. This is not colony abundance.
  • Abundance/density: a belt transect counts organisms over an area. 50 m × 5 m = 250 m2; 15 fish / 250 = 0.06 fish/m2. Species/size data help estimate biomass or erosion.
  • Benthic composition: photo quadrats archive sampled surfaces for annotation. Clear images and a consistent footprint matter; hidden organisms are missed.
  • Complexity: chain-and-tape rugosity = contoured distance / straight span. 5 m / 2.5 m = 2; a flat surface gives 1. The handbook's reversed wording is inconsistent with its stated interpretation.
  • Colour/bleaching condition: CoralWatch compares lightest/darkest patches against a chart. It is a pigment-related proxy, not a direct measure of mortality or carbonate production.
  • Shape/growth: SfM builds a scaled 3D model from overlapping images. A ruler sets real-world scale; complete coverage and repeatable processing matter. 1,320 − 1,200 = 120 cm3/year over one year, not automatically 120 g of skeleton.

VBS versus ED50 versus DHW: VBS is the ordinal visual bleaching score 1-5; ED50 is the fitted treatment temperature at the practical's midpoint VBS 3; DHW is accumulated environmental heat exposure. With VBS = −14.5 + 0.5T, set score to 3: T = (3 + 14.5)/0.5 = 35°C. That is not 50% mortality or a counted 50% loss of algae.

Good comparison: comparable habitat, effort, timing, visibility and methods; independent replicates; baseline and controls. Many fragments in one temperature tank do not replicate tanks: a tank effect can be mistaken for a temperature effect. Calibration fits a model; validation tests it with independent observations.

The remaining lecture material

Mapping and remote sensing

A raster is a pixel grid; vector data are points, lines or polygons. A datum supplies a positional reference; a projection puts curved Earth on a flat map. Georeferencing aligns real-world locations. Passive sensors record existing energy, such as reflected sunlight; active LiDAR/sonar emit a signal and measure returns.

The image combines effects of atmosphere, surface, water and bottom. Depth, turbidity, shadows and sun glint can mimic habitat differences; red light attenuates faster than blue/green. A 10 m pixel covers 100 m2 and may mix coral, sand and algae. Choose classes/resolution, correct the imagery, train with known examples, then validate independently. Fine pixels increase detail but do not guarantee accurate labels.

Marine plants and blue carbon

Green algae have chlorophyll a/b; brown algae have a/c and fucoxanthin; red algae have accessory pigments such as phycoerythrin. A brown-algal holdfast attaches tissue rather than acting as a root. Turf is a short mixed assemblage; Halimeda is calcifying green algae; CCA is calcifying red algae. Seagrasses are flowering plants with roots; algae are not small seagrasses.

Primary production is new organic carbon per time. Chlorophyll a is an indirect biomass indicator, not a species count. Some cyanobacteria fix nitrogen gas into usable nitrogen compounds; this is not photosynthetic carbon fixation.

Blue carbon is carbon stored in coastal systems such as seagrasses/mangroves. Seagrass traps particles and buries organic material. The course figure 140 Mg C/ha in the top metre of soil means 140 metric tonnes already stored per hectare, accumulated over centuries/millennia, not each year. Two hectares at that density contain 280 tonnes C in that layer. Protection can prevent loss; new credit claims need additional benefit beyond a baseline, durable storage and monitoring.

Local threats and management

Sediment reduces light, smothers surfaces and impairs settlement; nutrients can stimulate algae; sewage/contaminants have further effects. Treat runoff/wastewater and coordinate coastal uses. Kaneohe Bay illustrates recovery after sewage diversion. In the Singapore example, large-scale colony relocation had under 11% survival: moving corals without suitable conditions is not sufficient.

Overharvesting alters food webs; blast fishing breaks framework, trawling damages seabed, cyanide harms organisms, and ghost gear continues trapping animals. Low densities can reduce fertilisation in broadcast spawners such as harvested sea cucumbers. COTS are native coral predators: outbreaks, not mere presence, cause major damage. Nutrient-enhanced larval food/survival is one proposed contributor.

Groundings combine physical damage and pollution. Ballast water transports organisms; hull biofouling is another pathway. Lionfish's western Atlantic introduction is linked to aquarium releases, not established here as a ballast case. Antifouling reduces hull growth but toxic products can harm non-target organisms. Tourism can fund conservation but anchoring, trampling, sewage and wildlife disturbance require permits, moorings, education and enforcement. MPAs cannot stop global warming.

Historical GBR statistic: coral cover declined 50.7% in 1985-2012. Of the loss, 48% was attributed to cyclones, 42% to COTS and 10% to bleaching. These are shares of the loss during that period, not today's condition or percentages to add to 50.7.

Marginal versus extreme versus degraded

In the course distinction, extreme concerns unusual environmental conditions; marginal concerns ecological characteristics, such as low reef-building capacity; degraded means decline from an earlier baseline. Naturally low cover does not prove damage. Stradbroke communities persisted episodically over about 7,000 years, yet recent water-quality deterioration also changed them: natural persistence and recent degradation can coexist.

Extreme sites can reveal stress tolerance or act as natural analogues. A CO2 seep resembles some acidification conditions; mesophotic reefs occur deeper in low light. Neither reproduces every future stress. A refugium gives relative protection, not permanent immunity.

Safety and reef configurations

Know each hazard's mechanism: nematocyst discharge; coral cuts/infection; urchin spines; cone-snail venomous tooth; fish venomous spines; ray puncture; triggerfish bites. Avoid contact. Jellyfish treatment is species-specific; do not learn one vinegar/warm-water rule for all stings. The safety section links the corrected guidance.

Fringing reefs adjoin shore; barrier reefs have a substantial intervening lagoon; patch reefs are isolated patches; atolls form rings around lagoons. The classic atoll model involves growth around a subsiding volcanic island, modified by sea-level and erosion history.

What a strong written answer contains

Example: losing grazers reduces algal consumption, allowing algae to occupy settlement space and inhibit coral recruitment. Jamaican Diadema loss illustrates this mechanism. However, grazers also erode carbonate, so more grazing does not automatically mean a more positive carbonate budget.

Your chosen oral / Restoration

Defend Reef Restoration

Your task is to persuade a scientist who thinks active and passive restoration are pointless and carbon-emissions policies should receive the emphasis. You must advocate restoration and support your case with studies, not simply describe techniques.

Format: three-minute speech, then two minutes of questions. One A4 handwritten prompt sheet; no PowerPoint. Marks assess knowledge, argument, evidence, delivery and responses.

Your position

Emissions reduction is indispensable, but it is not a substitute for restoring damaged reefs. Targeted restoration can rebuild depleted coral populations and ecological functions while pressure reduction improves their chance of lasting. Defend that qualified claim, not "we can plant our way out of climate change".

The evidence to understand before speaking

1. Laughing Bird Caye: active restoration can persist

Carne and colleagues reported 17 of 19 original A. palmata transplants surviving from 2006 to May 2016: about 89% over ten years, plus spawning by nursery-grown outplants. This demonstrates persistence and reproductive potential.

Limit: a small cohort at one protected site is not a universal survival rate. Spawning does not prove recruitment. The authors associate success with protection and community involvement, without experimentally isolating their contributions. Carne et al., GCFI proceedings (2017).

2. Grazing and water quality: make recovery possible

The Hughes et al. (2007) exclusion experiment shows algae accumulates when grazing fish are excluded. Protecting grazing processes therefore addresses a barrier to coral recruitment. Kaneohe Bay's sewage-diversion example makes the complementary point: removing a damaging pressure can enable recovery. Neither example proves that every MPA or sewage project alone will restore a reef; use them to explain why passive measures accompany planting.

3. Tolerance varies, so restoration design matters

Ofu demonstrates an environmental component of heat tolerance; the lecture's common-garden results show some persistence of high tolerance rankings. Use this to justify testing candidates and retaining diversity, not promising permanent "super corals". The 40% top-quartile/70% top-half findings are labelled in preparation and should not be presented as settled universal evidence.

4. The strongest objection: cost and scale

Bayraktarov et al. (2019) systematically reviewed restoration outcomes and costs. The lecture's figure gives direct-transplantation median cost around $218,000/ha in 2010 US dollars, and many projects have only one or two years of follow-up. Costs and monitoring gaps justify selective investment and rigorous evaluation, not the claim that no project can help. Review; the course chart figures and scale calculation.

A three-minute speech

About 390 words. Rehearse aloud; the time limit, not a word-count estimate, determines whether it fits.

Reducing carbon emissions is essential to the future of coral reefs. But that does not make restoration pointless. My argument is that targeted restoration and emissions reduction solve different parts of the same problem: one limits future damage, while the other helps rebuild ecosystems already depleted.

Passive restoration removes local barriers to recovery, such as poor water quality and damaging fishing. Active restoration directly assists recovery through transplanting corals, nursery propagation or supplying larvae. The objective should not be a collection of living fragments, but a reef that grows, reproduces and supports ecological functions.

First, there is evidence that active restoration can persist. At Laughing Bird Caye in Belize, Carne and colleagues reported that 17 of 19 original elkhorn coral transplants survived for ten years. They also documented spawning by nursery-grown outplants. That is evidence of persistence and reproductive potential, beyond first-year survival. It is not proof that all reefs can achieve the same outcome, or that spawning alone guarantees recruitment.

Second, cutting emissions does not stop overfishing or local pollution. These pressures also need action. In Hughes and colleagues' experiment, keeping grazing fish out of reef patches allowed algae to build up. That algae can take over surfaces where young corals would otherwise settle. Protecting algae-eating fish therefore helps keep space available for new corals. This is why restoration includes removing local pressures, not just planting: it helps both transplanted corals and naturally arriving young corals establish.

Third, we should rebuild for future conditions rather than simply recreate a vulnerable past community. That means testing heat tolerance and retaining multiple species, growth forms and genotypes. Fast-growing branching corals can restore structure, but relying on one clone or one vulnerable type creates shared risk. Screening helps select candidates; it does not guarantee immunity to future heatwaves.

The objection about cost and scale is serious. The course's review figure puts median direct-transplantation costs at roughly 218,000 US dollars per hectare in 2010 prices, and many projects are monitored for only one or two years. Restoration cannot replace emissions policy or cheaply rebuild every reef. We should target suitable sites, compare outcomes with unrestored areas, and monitor survival, reproduction, recruitment and ecosystem function over years.

My conclusion is therefore not restoration instead of emissions reduction. It is rapid emissions reduction, local pressure reduction and evidence-led restoration together. Dismissing restoration altogether would discard a tool that can rebuild populations and functions at sites where its benefits are demonstrated.

Prepare for the two-minute questioning

Why restore reefs if another heatwave could kill them?

That risk is real and should influence site selection, tolerance screening and investment. Restoration is justified where benefits have a credible chance of persisting; it does not justify planting everywhere. Emissions reduction remains necessary to improve long-term prospects.

Why not spend all the money on emissions reduction?

Emissions reduction addresses the global driver but does not directly replace depleted local populations or lost habitat. Whether a particular restoration project deserves funding depends on its additional ecological benefit, cost and alternatives. I am defending targeted projects, not claiming they are always the best investment.

What if the reef would recover without planting?

Then passive measures may be sufficient and active planting may add little. Use comparable unrestored sites and baseline measurements to estimate the additional benefit. Active assistance is most defensible when evidence identifies a recovery bottleneck it can address.

How would you measure success?

Track survival, growth, reproduction and new juvenile recruitment, plus diversity, habitat complexity and carbonate production. Compare with baseline/unrestored sites over several years, including after disturbances. Planting counts and first-year survival alone do not show self-sustaining recovery.

Why not plant only fast-growing or heat-resistant corals?

One trait does not represent every function or threat. Fast growth can coexist with vulnerability; heat tolerance need not confer disease resistance or good reproduction. Mixed species, growth forms and genotypes spread risk and provide different habitat functions.

Are a thousand fragments a thousand different genotypes?

Not necessarily. Fragments from one source colony are clones, apart from changes such as mutations. Increase genetic diversity by using multiple distinct source genotypes, not merely many pieces.

Does evidence of spawning prove the restored reef is self-sustaining?

No. Gametes still need to fertilise, larvae must settle, and juveniles must survive. Spawning shows reproductive potential; successful recruitment and persistence need separate monitoring.

What does the common-garden result tell you?

Some tolerance rankings persisted in a shared environment: 40% of the original top quartile stayed top quartile and 70% stayed top half. It supports testing/selection, not permanent immunity or a uniquely genetic explanation. The lecture labels this work in preparation.

What are the risks of moving corals?

Disease transfer, destination mismatch and changes to local genetic/ecological relationships. A heat-tolerant donor may perform poorly under different light, sediment or other conditions. Assess biosecurity, environmental match, permissions and field performance.

What is your strongest evidence, and its limitation?

The Belize report supplies multi-year persistence and observed spawning, whereas many projects only report early survival. Its small, selected original cohort and protected-site setting limit generalisation. It is evidence that restoration can work, not that it always works.

Keep the argument disciplined: concede real limits, then explain the conditions under which restoration adds value. Do not turn a survival percentage into ecosystem recovery, a heat-test ranking into immunity, or one successful site into a global guarantee.
